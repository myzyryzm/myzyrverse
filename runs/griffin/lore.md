# The Griffin of Crete

## Summary
- The griffin is a real, natural animal in this world. It is flightless and wingless: a large cat in body and build, with a raptor's head and beak and an upright crest.
- It was never magical, never ridden, and never touched by purple.
- It once ranged widely. Men harnessed it for war and for chariot-pulling, and it was the guardian animal of the Minoan palaces.
- By 1160 most people believe it is extinct. The Throne Room paintings at Knossos and a few old objects are taken to be its only remaining traces.
- In fact a remnant survives in the high country of Mount Ida on Crete, and perhaps elsewhere. Drought-driven hunger is now pushing it down toward people.
- During his Sherden training on Crete, Jon finds a single orphaned juvenile. It bonds to him, and to him alone, as a young animal bonds to a parent. It goes with him on his journey through Greece and Anatolia.

## Canon conflicts
Nothing in this document contradicts prehistory canon. Four points of contact are worth noting.
- **Sherden training on Crete.** Canon says the training is *possibly* at Knossos. The brief and the accepted research place the discovery in the Cretan mountains during that training. This document therefore assumes Jon is on Crete when he finds the griffin. What the training consists of, and what the Minotaur trial is, stay open (q-ccb8185f).
- **The "griffin-rider" tomb.** One accepted proposal calls the Pylos grave a griffin-*rider*'s tomb. The author's note overrides this: griffins were never ridden. This document calls the dead man a **griffin-keeper**, a handler and charioteer of war-griffins.
- **The Cyclopes' expulsion.** Canon says men drove the Cyclopes out of the palaces, possibly thousands of years ago, and leaves open who did it and when (q-e0609000). The author's addition says men and their griffins fought an ancient war against the Cyclopes. This document treats that war as settled. It does **not** declare that the war *was* the expulsion; that link is listed as an open question.
- **Wings in art.** The accepted research settles the living animal as wingless. Most real Aegean art shows winged griffins. This document gives an in-world reason for the mismatch (see "Body") and does not change the animal.

## The animal

### Settled
- **Nature:** an ordinary animal of flesh and appetite, like a lion or a vulture. No purple is involved in its biology, lifespan or senses.
- **Build:** a big cat in body, gait and behavior: lion-bodied and heavy-shouldered. The head and beak are those of a carrion raptor. It has an upright crest, like the Throne Room griffins.
- **No wings, no flight.** Its head is light for its size because it is built like a bird's, and its body has a big cat's power. That combination lets it **leap farther than any lion**: across ravines, from ledge to ledge, and onto walls.
- **Size:** a grown griffin is the size of a **cave lion**, larger than any lion living in 1160.
- **Uses in the past:** men harnessed griffins to pull chariots and loosed them in battle. **They were never ridden** like horses.
- **Senses the Drought.** Griffins sense the Drought's beasts before people can, and they react to them. This is a natural trait of the species.
- **Enmity with the Cyclopes.** Griffins are hostile to Cyclopes. The hostility is partly instinct and partly kept alive in human legend.
- **Bond with Jon.** Jon's griffin is young, orphaned, and bonded to Jon only.

### Body (proposed specifics)
- **Adult size:**
  - It stands at the shoulder about as high as a man's chest.
  - It weighs as much as four grown men.
  - Males are larger than females.
- **Leap:**
  - From a standing start, an adult can clear a gap of twelve to fifteen paces.
  - It can spring onto a ledge twice a man's height.
  - It cannot sustain a chase over open ground. It is an ambush hunter that drops from rocks.
- **Head:**
  - The skull is bird-light.
  - The beak is a vulture's hooked beak, built for tearing, and strong enough to crack long bones for the marrow, as the bearded vultures of Ida do.
  - Feathers cover the head and throat and give way to short fur at the nape.
- **Crest:**
  - A crest of stiff, quill-like feathers runs from the brow to the back of the skull. The griffin flattens it when calm and raises it when alarmed or about to attack.
  - This is the writers' main visible "tell".
- **Shoulder ruff:**
  - Adults grow a mane of long, layered feathers over the shoulders. They spread it when displaying to rivals or threats.
  - Aegean painters drew this display as wings. That is why most griffin art is winged and the Throne Room portraits, drawn from griffins at rest, are not.
- **Coat:**
  - Tawny-ochre with a pale belly.
  - Juveniles carry dark **spiral markings** on the shoulders and flanks, the spirals of Aegean art. These fade as the animal matures, as a lion cub's spots do.
  - A spiral-marked griffin is therefore visibly young.
- **Voice:**
  - Young griffins chirp and trill, like the nestlings on the Lefkandi vessel.
  - Adults have a rasping shriek and a deep chest-purr. They do not roar.
- **Diet:**
  - Strict meat-eaters: wild goat (the Cretan agrimi), deer, sheep, carrion and bone.
  - Because they can live on bones and old carcasses, the last griffins survived lean years that would have starved a lion.
  - A grown griffin needs roughly one goat every three days.
- **Life cycle:**
  - Pairs mate for life and raise their young together.
  - Young stay with their parents for about two years.
  - A griffin reaches full size at about four years.
  - Lifespan in the wild is about twenty-five years.
- **Temperament:**
  - Territorial, and shy of people when it can afford to be.
  - Fiercely protective of its young or its family group.
  - An orphan attaches to whatever creature takes the parent's place.

## History of the species

### Before the palaces
- Wild griffins once ranged from the mountains of the far north-east, across Anatolia and northern Greece, to Crete.
- **The ancient war:** long before the Minoan and Mycenaean palaces, men who kept griffins fought the Cyclopes.
  - In that war griffins fought beside men as hunting and war beasts, loosed against the giants.
  - The chariot came later.
- Defeated Cyclopes scattered across northern Greece, into Anatolia and out to the steppe.
- Human legend and griffin instinct both remember the war.

### The palace griffins of Crete
- The Minoans kept griffins at Knossos and the other palaces in two roles:
  - **sacred animals of the palace priestesses**, kept beside the goddess's seat and harnessed to her chariot in rites;
  - **guardians of the ruler's house**.
- Griffins were harnessed to war chariots and ceremonial chariots. The Hagia Triada sarcophagus shows griffins drawing a goddess's chariot.
- The **Throne Room paintings** at Knossos are portraits of the real animals: couchant, wingless and crested, among papyrus.
  - The surviving pair is on the west wall, flanking the doorway into an inner room. They do not flank the throne, as later restorers assumed.
  - The painters showed the animals as they actually lay on guard.
- Griffins were bred and handled by specialist keepers. Keeping was hereditary craft knowledge and was never written down.

### Egypt and the mainland
- **Egypt** knew the griffin as the *srf*. In Egyptian thought it carried royal and solar meaning, close to the sphinx and to the king's *ka*.
- A crested Aegean griffin appears on the ceremonial axe of **Ahmose**, the king who drove out the Hyksos. In Egypt the griffin is therefore a sign of **expelling foreign rulers**.
- At **Avaris**, beside the later site of Pi-Ramesses, a Thutmosid palace had Minoan-painted walls that included a winged, crested griffin hunting beside a lion and a leopard.
  - The canon capital stands on ground once decorated with griffins.
  - Whether a living griffin ever reached the Egyptian court is not settled.
- **Mycenaean lords** took the griffin over as an emblem of the *wanax*. The **Griffin Warrior** buried at Pylos around 1450 BCE, about three centuries before the story, was a griffin-keeper: a man of 30–35 buried with an ivory griffin plaque between his legs and Minoan-made goods.
  - Whether keeping survived until the Mycenaean palaces fell is open.

### After the fall of Knossos
- When the palace at Knossos fell, one to two centuries before the story, its griffins escaped or were turned loose. They went feral in the **Psiloritis (Mount Ida) massif**, in gorges, cliffs and high caves that lowlanders did not search.
- With the cult and the keepers gone, the knowledge died. The paintings decayed or were buried, and Cretans came to treat griffins as creatures of the old gods: painted, not real.
- The surviving population was always small. It held on in the same high country where vultures still nest.

### The Drought
- Over the last ~50 years the Drought has thinned the wild goats and deer of the uplands and dried the high pastures.
- On Crete it is driving the last griffins downhill toward flocks, shepherds and the upland refuge villages, as it is driving the Cyclopes out of their caves on the mainland.
- The griffin is a **casualty** of the Drought, not a cure for it.
  - One griffin can kill one of the Drought's beasts.
  - No griffin, or number of griffins, can stop the Drought.

## Status in 1160
- **Exceedingly rare, not extinct.** Almost everyone believes the griffin is gone. Educated Egyptians, priests and Mycenaean lords know it only from art, heirlooms and old stories.
- **Crete:** a remnant on Ida, few enough to count on two hands. The author has fixed the number of griffins *on the page* at one for now.
- **Mainland Greece:** a few may survive in remote pockets. The Lefkandi alabastron, painted on Euboea about 1150–1125 BCE, shows a griffin pair feeding chirping young in a nest. In this world it reads as the work of someone who **saw** a living griffin family.
- **The far north-east:** steppe traders tell of griffins in the gold-bearing mountains beyond the north wind, still at war with one-eyed thieves. In this world the tradition is old and passed down by word of mouth.

## Jon's griffin

### Settled
- **One griffin, a juvenile, an orphan.** It has no parents or siblings left alive on Crete.
- Found during Jon's Sherden training in the Cretan mountains.
- Jon earns the bond by **protecting it, not taming it**. The decisive moment is his refusal to kill or cage it when Sherden practice demands exactly that.
- **A Bucephalus moment:** Jon calms it by noticing what frightens it, something everyone else has missed.
- **A family bond, not a royal one.**
  - The griffin does not attach to Jon because he is a pharaoh's son.
  - It attaches because he takes the place of the parent it lost.
  - Bred long ago to guard kings, it now guards its own: Jon, and in time the people Jon protects.
- **One voice, many years.**
  - The bond grows through daily handling and Jon's voice, in the manner of eagle-hunters, adapted to an animal that is more cat than bird.
  - Early in the journey the griffin is a mouth to feed and a danger to everyone near it. It becomes a real ally only as it grows.
- **Jon's alone.** No one else ever wins its trust: not refugees, not the Medjay farm boy, not family.
- **Never ridden, never dosed.** The griffin is not a mount and not a weapon of the purple.

### The finding (proposed)
- **Age and size at finding:**
  - About one year old, the size of a large hound.
  - Spiral markings bold, crest only beginning to stiffen.
  - It can already leap a man's height.
- **How it is orphaned:**
  1. The Ida griffins, starved by the Drought, have been taking sheep from an upland refuge village.
  2. The Sherden company on Crete is sent to kill the "lion", which also serves as a training hunt.
  3. Recruits kill the adult pair in a gorge den. One adult kills a Sherden-born recruit before it dies.
  4. A juvenile is found alive in the den.
- **The order:** the instructor orders the juvenile killed, or crated as a trophy for Pharaoh. Jon refuses.
- **The fear Jon notices:** the juvenile goes wild at the sight of the **Sherden horned helmets**, the horns of the men who killed its parents. Jon takes his helmet off, sits down below its eye level and talks until it stops shrieking.
- **Keeping it:** as eagle-hunters keep a young bird in the dark, Jon keeps the juvenile in a covered crate or a dark storeroom, perhaps inside the run-down palace. He talks to it every day so that his voice becomes its anchor.
- **Recognition:** the griffin is identified when someone, perhaps the drillmaster, perhaps Jon himself, matches it to the **Throne Room wall**.

### Growth across the journey (proposed)
| Time after finding | Size and capability | What it means on the road |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6 months | Hound-sized, clumsy, spiral-marked; chirps | A secret to hide and a mouth to feed; useless in a fight |
| 6–18 months | Leopard-sized; leaps ravines; first kills of its own | Hunts its own food, and refugees' goats; first warnings of the Drought's beasts |
| 18–36 months | Lion-sized; crest and ruff grown; spirals fading | A real guard; can kill a drought-runner; warlords notice it |
| ~4 years | Full cave-lion size | Strong enough to pull a chariot, if anyone dares harness it |

### What it does on the road
- **Early warning:** it raises its crest, refuses a road, or slips off hunting before the Drought's beasts appear. Jon's growing name as a prophet-leader rests partly on reading the animal correctly.
- **Cyclopes:** at the scent of a Cyclops it bristles, and either flees or attacks. Hill folk who still tell the old war-stories recognize what they are seeing.
- **Hunger:** it eats the refugees' animals and, when starving, may take a person. Jon will defend it, and that is part of his flaw: "I am the only one who can keep it alive."
- **A mirror of Jon:** it was bred to guard a palace that no longer exists, and it guards a column of refugees instead.

## How others read the griffin
- **Sherden instructors and the drillmaster:**
  - a trophy for Pharaoh;
  - a danger to the company;
  - after the coup, a lever for keeping the prince where he is.
- **Sherden-born recruits:** the bought prince gets the wonder. Their contempt turns to envy, all the sharper if one of their own died in the den.
- **Cretan upland villagers:**
  - a stock-killer;
  - the "old guardians" come back;
  - an omen they would rather not have.
- **Mycenaean warlords:**
  - a living mark of the *wanax*;
  - either a prize to take or a reason to bow to the boy who keeps it.
- **Egyptian priests and court:**
  - the *srf*, a royal and solar sign;
  - something the temples will claim the sole right to verify, as they verify the Apis bull.
- **Ramesses III**, while alive: the omen his reign was missing, a creature Ramesses the Great never had.
- **Pentawer's court:** a legitimate son walking home with a throne-guardian is a living Throne Room painting. It must be claimed, discredited or killed.
- **Isis:** proof of her line's worth, and a weapon for Tyre against Assyria.
- **Aegon:** someone died so that Jon could have a legend, while someone died for Aegon and left him a disgrace.

## Departures from the historical record
- Griffins are real animals here. Historically they are a motif of art and myth.
- The Knossos Throne Room griffins (painted Late Minoan II, long before 1160) are treated as portraits of living animals.
- The Lefkandi Griffin Alabastron, real and roughly contemporary with the story, is treated as drawn from life.
- The Griffin Warrior of Pylos, a real burial, is reinterpreted as a griffin-keeper.
- The Greek tradition of gold-guarding griffins and the one-eyed Arimaspians is first written down about 700 years after the story. Here it is an older tradition passed down by word of mouth.

## Proposed open questions
- Is Jon's griffin male or female? Females are smaller; eagle-hunters choose females because in eagles they are the larger sex.
- What does Jon call the griffin, and in what language: Egyptian, Minoan or Greek?
- Where exactly is it found: a gorge den on Ida, a high cave, or the deep levels under the Knossos ruins?
- Do the Sherden on Crete already know that griffins survive on Ida, and have they kept it secret?
- Does the finding take place during the Minotaur trial, or on a separate exercise?
- Is the fear-trigger in the Bucephalus moment the Sherden's horned helmets, the smell of purple-worked bronze, or something else?
- Who killed the parents: Sherden recruits, villagers, raiders or the Drought's beasts?
- Does a Sherden-born recruit die in the den, giving Jon's story a death that parallels the one in Aegon's?
- Was the ancient war of men and griffins against the Cyclopes the event that drove the Cyclopes out of the palaces?
- Are the one-eyed gold-thieves of steppe legend scattered Cyclopes?
- Did griffin-keeping survive among the Mycenaeans until their palaces fell, or end with Knossos?
- Did a living griffin ever reach the Egyptian court, for example with a Minoan embassy at Avaris?
- How many griffins survive on Ida, and does Jon ever learn of others there?
- Does a griffin family survive on Euboea or elsewhere on the mainland, as the Lefkandi vessel implies?
- Does the griffin die before the end of Jon's arc (the Enkidu path), outlive him, or is it set free?
- How does the griffin physically sense the Drought's beasts: by smell, by sound, or by something else?
- Is the griffin ever harnessed to a chariot in the story?

## Hooks
- Jon's first good look at the griffin comes in the ruined Throne Room, where he sees the same crested head painted on the wall.
- The drillmaster uses the griffin as a reason, or an excuse, to keep the prince on Crete when news of the coup arrives.
- The Sherden-born recruit who died in the den becomes the corps' grievance against Jon and the beast.
- Upland villagers who once left offerings to "the old guardians" shelter Jon, or turn him in to stop the killing of their sheep.
- A refuge village on Euboea, its potters painting griffin families from life, tells Jon he is not raising the last of its kind.
- At the Griffin Warrior's tomb at Pylos, Jon learns what griffin-keepers were and how young they died.
- A Pylian warlord demands the griffin as tribute, in the way of the white-elephant wars. Refusing means a fight; giving it means submission.
- The griffin's crest rises on an empty road, Jon turns the column back, and the drought-runners come the next day. That is the beginning of his reputation as a prophet.
- The griffin and a starving Cyclops meet in a Greek gorge. Hill folk recognize the ancient enmity before Jon does.
- The refugees go hungry while the griffin eats their goats. The protector's arrogance becomes a crisis of leadership.
- Pentawer's court hears rumors of a griffin and must claim it, mock it as a painted fable, or send killers.
- Isis learns of the griffin and wants it for Tyre's war, and Jon resists her for the same reasons he is like her.
- Jon brings a living *srf* to Pi-Ramesses, built beside griffin-painted Avaris. It evokes Ahmose's griffin axe and the expulsion of foreign rulers, and it arrives just as an Assyrian princess sits beside the throne.
- Egyptian priests insist on verifying the animal, Apis-fashion, which makes the temples kingmakers.
- Someone tries to dose the griffin with purple to make a war-beast of it, and the natural animal suffers for it.
- A full-grown griffin draws a war chariot for the first time in centuries.
- At the end of the arc, Jon sets the griffin free, as the eagle-hunter does, and hides so it will not follow him home.
