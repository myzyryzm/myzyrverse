## Background (context)

### Antiquarian: Bronze Age grounding

#### Local sources
- The author's source folder holds only a placeholder file. It has no notes on griffins, Knossos or the Sherden, so everything below comes from web sources.

#### What the Aegean griffin looked like
- The standard Aegean griffin has a lion's body, a vulture's beak and raised wings drawn in profile, one behind the other. It often has a crest and spiral markings on the body. The spirals may come from Egyptian falcon markings, and whether the creature began in Egypt, the Aegean or the Levant is still unresolved.
- The earliest Aegean griffins are on Middle Minoan II seals from Malia and Phaistos, roughly four to five centuries before the story.
- Its roles in art include a predator in hunting scenes (the West House miniatures at Akrotiri), a large griffin standing beside a goddess (Xeste 3, Akrotiri), and griffins pulling a chariot that carries two female figures on the Hagia Triada sarcophagus.
- From the Aegean the type spread into Cypro-Aegean and Mitannian seals and into 14th-century Levantine ivories.

#### The Knossos Throne Room griffins
- The Throne Room paintings are dated to Late Minoan II. Recent scholarship reads the program as an expression of a new *wanax* (king) ideology that combined older Minoan elements with new ones.
- The griffins are **wingless** and **couchant** (lying down), with an upright crest. They were painted mostly in red on white, with cross-hatching and ochre details, among hybrid papyrus-reed plants, some topped with a lotus-papyrus motif adapted from Egypt. Winglessness is very rare in Aegean art.
- Correction to the popular image: the surviving griffins are on the **west wall, flanking a doorway** into an inner room. They do not flank the throne. East of the throne only one couchant griffin's paw survives, and the matching griffin on the throne's west side is a modern guess. The Gilliérons' reconstructions, which most people picture, combined fragments from different paintings.
- A Late Minoan I seal impression from Knossos has been reconstructed as two overlapping couchant, wingless griffins among papyrus. Wingless griffins also appear in the palace at Pylos.
- At Pylos, a griffin-and-lion painting is placed behind the throne in the throne room.

#### Griffins in Egypt and at Avaris
- The Egyptian word for griffin is *srf/sfr*. One argument holds that the falcon-headed Egyptian griffin was a form of Horus and a variant of the sphinx standing for the king's *ka*: royal and solar symbolism. *Akhekh* is often given as another Egyptian griffin or dragon word, but the identification is shaky.
- A griffin in Aegean style, with crest and wings, appears on the ceremonial axe of Ahmose, the king who drove out the Hyksos.
- At Tell el-Dab'a (ancient Avaris) in the eastern Delta, a Thutmosid palace had Minoan-style frescoes: bull-leaping, hunts, and a Hunt Frieze with a crested, spiral-marked, winged griffin hunting alongside a lion and a leopard. The artists are argued to have been Minoans, perhaps sent through royal gift exchange. Bietak has suggested the frescoes marked a marriage to a Minoan princess.
- The site of Avaris lies beside the later royal city of Pi-Ramesses, which is the canon capital.

#### The Griffin Warrior and the griffin after the palaces
- The Griffin Warrior tomb at Pylos, of about 1450 BCE, gets its name from an ivory plaque of a griffin found between the dead man's legs. The tomb was full of Minoan-made objects and imagery.
- **A griffin image contemporary with the story:** the Griffin Alabastron from Lefkandi (Euboea), from a destruction level of about 1150–1125 BCE. Two adult griffins feed **chirping young in a nest** between them, and the female touches the male's arm with her paw. It is painted white on black.
- One reading of the Lefkandi vessel sees a 'domesticated' reworking of the palace throne griffins: these griffins guard their own young, not a king's palace.

#### The state of Knossos and Crete around 1160
- The palace's final fiery destruction is disputed. Many date it to early Late Minoan IIIA2 (14th century); Hallager and others argue for a later LM IIIB fire. Hallager's 2025 study suggests the palace's last phase was largely used for storage.
- Across Crete, people had been moving away from the coast to defensible upland sites since LM IIIB. Piracy after the fall of the Mycenaean palaces is one proposed cause. Karphi, a mountain refuge above the Lasithi plateau, was occupied from the crisis of about 1200 BCE to the early 10th century.
- In real history, then, the Throne Room in 1160 had been a ruin or a storeroom for one to two centuries. Its frescoes were buried or decaying, and life on the island had moved to the hills.

#### Animals behind the griffin
- **Lions:** there is no evidence that lions lived on Bronze Age Crete apart from pictures of them, which are common on Cretan seals. The motif was probably borrowed from Egypt and the Near East. On the Greek mainland, scattered lion bones (for example a heel bone near Tiryns) are debated: they could come from a wild population or from imported pelts and trophies.
- **Vultures:** the griffin's beak is a vulture's beak. Crete today holds the last breeding bearded vultures (lammergeier) in the Balkans, centred on the Psiloritis (Mount Ida) massif. Griffon vultures are found almost everywhere on the island.

#### The Sherden as Egyptian sources show them
- They wear horned helmets, usually with a disc or knob on top, and carry round shields with spears, daggers or two-edged swords, possibly of the Naue II type. Ramesses II took captured Sherden into his personal guard after defeating them early in his reign, and they appear in his Kadesh inscriptions.
- Later Sherden were allowed to settle under Egyptian rule, and a source of about 1100 BCE places Sherden in Canaan.

#### Later Greek griffin lore (after the story's date)
- From the 5th century BCE, Greek writers (Aeschylus, and Herodotus reporting a lost poem by Aristeas) placed gold-guarding griffins in the far north-east, fighting the one-eyed Arimaspians. Proposed locations range from the Carpathians to the Urals and Central Asia.
- The popular theory that Protoceratops fossils inspired the griffin has been criticised: the fossil beds lie hundreds of kilometres from the gold deposits.

### Historian: historical analogues

#### The Minoan griffin in the real record
- The Throne Room at Knossos dates to the 15th century BCE and is often called the oldest throne room in Europe. A pair of painted griffins lies on either side of the alabaster seat, set among papyrus reeds.
- Unlike most griffins in Bronze Age art, these are wingless and crested. Evans called the wingless throne-room griffin unique in the history of art. Modern scholars still call the missing wings unusual.
- Nobody agrees on what the room was for. Readings include a priest's or priestess's ritual seat, a seat for an epiphany of the goddess, and, more recently, an expression of the ideology of the wanax (king). In each reading the griffins are sacred attendants, not prey or monsters.
- The Hagia Triada sarcophagus (c. 1400 BCE) shows two female figures, probably goddesses, in a chariot drawn by griffins. On the opposite end the same kind of chariot is drawn by horses or goats. Evans noted that griffins are swapped in for horses the same way on Cypro-Minoan pottery.
- Mycenaean Greeks took the emblem over. The Griffin Warrior of Pylos (c. 1450 BCE) was a Mycenaean burial full of Minoan-style goods, with an ivory griffin plaque laid between his legs.
- The griffin reached Egypt too. At Tell el-Dab'a (Avaris) in the eastern Delta, the site next to which Pi-Ramesses was later built, Minoan-style frescoes include a hunt frieze with a crested, winged Aegean griffin beside a lion and a leopard. A similar Aegean griffin decorates the ceremonial axe of Ahmose, founder of the New Kingdom. Scholars still argue over whether Minoan artists painted these during a state visit or a royal gift exchange.

#### Analogue: the Lazarus taxon (the coelacanth, 1938)
- What happened: coelacanths were thought to have died out about 66 million years ago. In December 1938 a museum curator, Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, noticed a strange fish in a trawler's catch. An ichthyologist named it after her.
- How it works: species that seem to vanish and later turn up again are called Lazarus taxa. They usually survive in small ranges and places where fossils rarely form, such as deep water off steep volcanic slopes.
- What drove the people involved: the find was made by a junior person who happened to be looking in the right place. The credit, the specimen and the science then went to whoever had the authority to name and keep it.
- What transfers: survival in an overlooked pocket nobody searches, and the question of who gets to claim the animal once it is found.

#### Analogue: Yongle's giraffe, a beast as proof of a usurper's right to rule (Ming China, 1414)
- What happened: the Yongle Emperor took the throne after his nephew, the legitimate emperor Jianwen, died when the Nanjing palace burned. As a fourth son he needed to shore up his claim, and he rewrote the official history to leave Jianwen out.
- Mechanics: during Zheng He's voyages, envoys from Malindi sent a giraffe. Courtiers presented it as a qilin, one of the four auspicious beasts of Confucian tradition, whose appearance marks a virtuous ruler. They wrote verses celebrating it as an omen.
- What drove the people involved: a usurper needs heaven to sign off on his rule, and a rare animal is a sign anyone can see. Yongle himself publicly played down the qilin link, taking the orthodox Confucian line, while his court spread it for him.
- Outcome and caution: the omen helped make the reign look legitimate, though historians argue over how much weight the giraffe really carried.
- What transfers: a usurping court wants an omen-beast, or wants someone else's omen-beast discredited. Who owns the animal becomes part of the argument over who should rule.

#### Analogue: the War of the White Elephants (Burma and Siam, 1563–64)
- What happened: King Bayinnaung of Burma demanded two white elephants from King Maha Chakkraphat of Ayutthaya as tribute. Chakkraphat refused, and Burma invaded.
- Mechanics: in both cultures white elephants were sacred signs of a righteous king, so asking for one was a demand that the other king submit. Refusing meant defiance.
- Outcome: Burma won. Siam had to hand over more elephants than first demanded, give royal hostages including the young prince Naresuan, and become a vassal. Naresuan later grew up to win Siam's independence back.
- What transfers: a sacred animal as the stake in a war, demanded as tribute and impossible to give up without losing face. A royal boy taken in the same settlement later turns the tables.

#### Analogue: Charlemagne's elephant, a living gift on a long road (797–810)
- What happened: Caliph Harun al-Rashid sent an elephant, Abul-Abbas, to Charlemagne. Of the three Frankish envoys sent to Baghdad, only Isaac the Jew survived to bring it back.
- Mechanics: the trip took years, overland through Egypt, along the North African coast, by sea to Genoa, then over the Alps. Isaac reached Aachen in 802. The elephant died suddenly in 810 while Charlemagne was on campaign.
- What transfers: moving a huge rare animal is logistics as much as wonder. It has to be fed, sheltered, hidden and carried across water, and the person who escorts it through a broken world becomes famous for it.

#### Analogue: how a raptor bonds with a person (Kazakh and Kyrgyz eagle hunters)
- Mechanics: eaglets are taken from crevice nests and live for years with the hunter's family. At first they are hooded and caged on a swaying perch while the handler sings to them, so they learn his voice. Training takes about three to four years of daily work by one person.
- Females are taken because they grow larger. The trained bird answers only to her trainer's voice.
- Release: tradition sets the eagle free after about a decade, on a spring morning. The hunter sometimes hides or waits until dark so she won't follow him home. Sources disagree on the exact length of service, and some traditional hunters frown on keeping a bird more than seven or eight years.
- What transfers: the bond is earned slowly, through one voice and one hand, and it carries a built-in ending in which the bird goes free.

#### Analogue: Alexander and Bucephalus (c. 344 BCE)
- What happened: a dealer offered Philip II a horse for 13 talents, but it reared and would not let any of the king's men near it. The young Alexander bet he could tame it.
- Mechanics: he had noticed that the horse was frightened by the movement of its own shadow. He turned it to face the sun and calmed it. Philip told him Macedonia was too small for him.
- What transfers: a famous taming that comes from noticing something everyone else missed, not from force, and is read at once as a sign of the boy's destiny.

#### Analogue: the Apis bull, an animal that is an office (Egypt)
- Mechanics: only one Apis bull lived at a time, recognized by set markings. When it died, Egypt went into mourning comparable to a pharaoh's death, and priests searched the country for a successor with matching marks. The new bull was kept apart for forty days before being installed at Memphis.
- From Amenhotep III onward the bulls were buried in the Serapeum at Saqqara.
- What transfers: Egypt already has a working institution that treats a living animal as sacred, with priests who decide which animal is the real one. That makes the authority to verify a sacred beast a political lever.

#### Analogue: griffins and one-eyed men (the Arimaspi tradition)
- Greek writers from Aeschylus and Herodotus on described one-eyed Arimaspians in the far northeast who stole gold from the griffins guarding it.
- Adrienne Mayor proposed that the legend began with Protoceratops fossils found by gold prospectors. Paleontologists have since argued that the fossil beds lie too far from ancient gold sites, so the theory is contested.
- What transfers: an old mythic enmity between griffins and one-eyed giants, recorded in ancient sources and available to borrow.

### Mythographer: fictional analogues

#### The griffin in the real Bronze Age record (history, not fiction)
- **Knossos throne room.** Painted griffins flank the throne at Knossos. Evans read them as guardians, with one on each side of the doorway. One scholarly reading ties the room's decoration to the ideology of the *wanax* (king). Note: much of the fresco seen today is a modern reconstruction assembled from fragments, so its exact layout is uncertain.
- **The Griffin Warrior of Pylos.** A rich shaft grave near the Palace of Nestor, dated about 1450 BCE, held a man aged 30–35. He is nicknamed for an ivory plaque, found between his legs, that shows a griffin walking through rocky country. Many of the grave goods are Minoan in style.
- **Griffins on Egypt's doorstep.** Aegean-style wall paintings were found at Tell el-Dab'a (ancient Avaris, the old Hyksos capital in the eastern Delta, right beside Pi-Ramesses). They include a crested, winged griffin in a hunting frieze alongside lions and leopards. Scholars link them to diplomacy, royal marriage or gift exchange with the Aegean.
- **Ahmose's griffin axe.** A ceremonial axe from the burial of Queen Ahhotep carries a Minoan-style griffin and celebrates Ahmose's victories over the Hyksos. It is one of the earliest New Kingdom examples of a weapon awarded for military merit. So in Egypt the griffin was already a mark of royal, warlike legitimacy, and specifically of the expulsion of foreign rulers.
- **Gold-guarding griffins.** Greek tradition, through Aristeas and later Herodotus, puts gold-guarding griffins in the far north-east, fighting the one-eyed Arimaspians who try to steal the gold. Adrienne Mayor argued the legend grew from Protoceratops fossils seen by steppe gold-miners. Critics answer that the fossil beds lie far from ancient gold sites and that griffin imagery is millennia older than the Scythians.
- The real griffin is a composite: a lion's body with an eagle's head and wings, and often a crest. Real history has no living griffins. Their existence in this saga is a fantasy addition, in the same category as the canon Cyclopes and werewolves.

#### Ghost and the direwolves, A Song of Ice and Fire / Game of Thrones (fiction)
- **What happens.** The Stark children find a litter of direwolves, a species thought gone from the South, beside their dead mother. The sixth pup is an albino runt found apart from the others. Jon claims him after Theon suggests killing him. Martin describes Ghost as the silent one, "related to the other direwolves but one apart as well".
- **What the device does.** The animal is the hero's soul made visible. Ghost's otherness mirrors Jon's bastardy. Because he is silent, he can scout, warn and kill without stealing the protagonist's lines. A companion animal also gives a lonely POV character someone to be tender with.
- **Why it works.** Readers read loyalty to the animal as a measure of the hero's character. The mystery of the beasts' return also signals that the world's old magic is waking.
- **Pitfalls.** A companion this strong solves problems, so the author has to keep sending it offstage. On screen, Ghost all but disappeared, and Jon's curt goodbye to him in the final season was widely criticised as a betrayal of the bond. The lesson: if you introduce the creature, budget for it in every chapter, or write a reason it is absent.

#### Hiccup and Toothless, How to Train Your Dragon (fiction)
- **What happens.** A boy raised to kill dragons in a training arena downs a rare, feared dragon and finds it trapped, missing a tail fin. He builds it a prosthetic fin. Neither can fly without the other.
- **What the device does.** The director has said the injury was chosen to build a stronger bond. The wound makes the friendship structural, not just sentimental: "if one crashes down, so does the other". The arena gives the story an institution whose whole purpose is killing the creature, so befriending it is rebellion.
- **Why it works.** Mutual need beats a one-way rescue. The hero has to learn the animal's body before he can earn flight, and that learning is the training montage.
- **Pitfalls.** Too cute. The predator becomes a pet, and the danger evaporates. The "kill the beast, but the boy refuses" beat is now very familiar.

#### Buckbeak, Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban (fiction)
- **What happens.** A hippogriff must be bowed to, with eye contact held, before it can be approached. When the arrogant Draco insults it, it slashes him. His powerful father then gets a committee to condemn the creature to execution. The heroes free it, and it flies off carrying a fugitive.
- **What the device does.** The creature has its own etiquette. It judges people, and that reveals character: the humble are accepted and the entitled get hurt. Its legal persecution turns the creature into a political victim and a test of who in authority is corrupt.
- **Pitfalls.** The rescue relies on a magical reset. The creature then becomes a convenient escape vehicle and has little arc of its own.

#### Bellerophon and Pegasus; Alexander's griffin flight (myth and legend)
- **Bellerophon.** On a seer's advice he sleeps in Athena's temple and dreams she gives him a golden bridle. He finds Pegasus drinking at the spring of Peirene and bridles him. Later, swollen with success, he tries to ride to Olympus. Pegasus throws him (in one version Zeus sends a gadfly) and flies on alone.
- **Alexander Romance.** Late legend, not history. Alexander yokes griffins (birds of prey in earlier versions) to a basket or throne and steers them upward by holding meat on a spear just out of reach, until he is forced back down.
- **What these do.** The tamed sky-beast is power borrowed, not owned. Both stories punish the rider who treats the creature as a ladder to divinity. The Alexander image is especially sharp: control by perpetual hunger, never by trust.
- **Pitfalls.** The moral tale can feel preachy, and the creature serves only as a symbol.

#### Other useful echoes (fiction and myth)
- **Enkidu (Epic of Gilgamesh).** A wild being from the steppe is civilised, becomes the king's other half, and dies. His death drives the hero's maturity. This is the template for a companion whose death is the point.
- **Androcles and the lion (fable).** The hero removes a thorn, and the beast repays the debt in the arena years later. Mercy gets banked and returned.
- **Daenerys's dragons (ASOIAF).** "Extinct" creatures return and are read by everyone as a dynastic claim. The pitfalls are the chosen-one glow and the creature as an army-ending weapon. The more interesting material is Drogon's refusal to be controlled and the burned children.

## Proposals

### Where the guardians went: the palace griffins gone feral on Ida (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
The griffins were once real animals kept by the kings of Knossos as palace guardians. When the palace fell they escaped into the high country of Mount Ida, and people came to believe they had died out. Sherden recruits find one during training in the mountains, perhaps around the Minotaur trial.
Affects: Jon, the Sherden, Knossos, the Minotaur, the Drought, Cretan hill refuges

**World**: The Throne Room griffins flank a doorway into an inner room and belong to a program of royal power, the *wanax* ideology. In this world they are portraits of the palace's real guardian animals. By 1160 the palace has been a ruin or storehouse for one to two centuries, and Cretans have moved to upland refuges such as Karphi. The Psiloritis massif, where Crete's last bearded vultures nest today, is a natural habitat for a vulture-beaked hunter.

**Plot**: The Drought is already pushing the Cyclopes out of their caves (canon). It can push the last griffins down out of the high pastures in the same way, into contact with Sherden recruits. Upland villagers who leave offerings to 'the old guardians' could be the only people who knew the griffins survived.

**Characterization**: The friendship feels earned if Jon helps the griffin rather than tames it: for example, he refuses to kill or capture it when the Sherden curriculum calls for exactly that.

**Integration**: This fits the canon's run-down Knossos with the Minotaur still in it, and it reuses the Drought-displaces-creatures pattern already set up for the Cyclopes. It departs from real history on purpose: the griffins are made real, and the palace's guardian imagery is read as a record of real animals.

Questions this raises:
- Is the griffin found inside the palace ruins (an inner room, a crypt), or in the mountain caves of Ida? (unanswered)
- Do the Sherden instructors at Knossos already know griffins survive, and are they keeping it secret? (unanswered)

### The Lefkandi turn: griffins as parents, not palace guards (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Make the griffin's loyalty work like a family bond rather than a king's guardian bond. It attaches to Jon because he protects its young or its nest, not because he is royal.
Affects: Jon, the griffin, Greece, refugees on the exodus

**World**: Around 1150–1125 BCE, potters at Lefkandi painted griffin parents feeding their young, with the female's paw on the male's arm. One scholar reads this as the palace griffin 'domesticated' after the palaces fell: it now guards its own young, not a throne.

**Characterization**: This gives the friendship a theme that suits Jon's arc: an animal once bred to guard kings now guards its own young and its chosen people, while Jon, a king's son, becomes the protector of refugees.

**Plot**: Painted pottery or a shrine showing griffin families in a Greek refuge village could tell Jon that griffins have survived on the mainland too. That gives the journey a reason to go through particular places, or a reason for the griffin to turn aside.

**Integration**: The Lefkandi vessel is real and dates almost exactly to the story year. Treating it as evidence of living griffins in Greece is an invention. It fits the 'thought to have died out' brief, provided only a few people, such as hill villagers, know the truth.

Questions this raises:
- Is Jon's griffin an orphan, or are there parents or siblings he has to leave behind on Crete? -> Author's answer: orphan

### Northern kin: a thread to the Steppe (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Seed the idea that griffins also survive in the far north-east, guarding gold. This would give the undecided Steppe/Amazon thread a reason to meet Jon's journey through Anatolia.
Affects: the griffin, the Steppe, Anatolia, Jon's exodus

**World**: Classical Greek writers placed gold-guarding griffins near the north wind's lands, fighting one-eyed Arimaspians, with suggested locations from the Carpathians to Central Asia. The fossil explanation for this lore is weak, which leaves the author free to treat it as memory of real animals.

**Integration**: **Anachronism:** this lore is first recorded about 700 years after 1160 BCE. Here it would appear as an older oral tradition among steppe traders. One-eyed Arimaspians would also link neatly to the canon Cyclopes.
