# The War of Succession (1190–1185 BCE)

## Summary
- The War of Succession was a five-year civil war over the throne of Egypt, fought between two grandsons of Ramesses II. It began with the death of Ramesses II's son and ended when Setnakhte took the throne around 1185.
- The two claimants were brothers, both sons of the old king:
  - **Seti II**, the elder;
  - **Amenmesse**, the younger.
- When Amenmesse died, his son **Siptah** took up the claim, and the war became "two brothers plus a nephew."
- Siptah killed his uncle, Seti II. Seti II left no surviving heirs.
- **Setnakhte**, Seti II's top general, then defeated Siptah and avenged his master. Siptah died in that defeat.
- Setnakhte took the throne around 1185 and founded the 20th Dynasty. His son is Ramesses III.
- The war badly weakened the New Kingdom. It was fought in the same years that Ugarit and the northern ports fell (c. 1192–1190).

## The house of Ramesses II
- **Ramesses II "the Great"** (born ~1300, reigned 1275–1210) was brutal and very successful. His reign is the high-water mark of the New Kingdom.
- **His son, the old king** (reigned 1210–~1190; name not yet set; historical parallel Merneptah), ruled about twenty years with fair success.
  - He fathered both claimants.
  - He did **not** openly favour Amenmesse.
  - He did not settle the succession in a way that held after his death. His death started the war.
- **Setnakhte** was not a direct descendant of Ramesses II. He had some royal blood: "a second son of a second son of a third daughter."
  - During the war he was Seti II's top general.

## The brothers

### Seti II, the elder
- He was the legitimate heir by order of birth.
- He was not seen as having a warrior's instinct. Men who wanted a fighting king looked elsewhere.
- Many judged him hollow. He may not have been, and he may have been underestimated.
- Whatever made him seem unfit was **not vice**. Its exact nature is undecided.
  - His failing is meant to differ from Aegon's, so that the two echo each other in structure rather than copy each other.
- His strength was legitimacy. He held the capital and the established order.

### Amenmesse, the younger
- He was seen as more of a warrior than his brother.
- His support came largely from that warrior instinct: soldiers and officials who wanted an able king rallied to him.
- He believed the order of birth was an accident of the gods.
- He told himself he took what Egypt needed, not what he wanted.
- His grievance was not a matter of paternal favour, because his father never openly preferred him. It was a conviction about ability against birth.
- At their father's death he was **Viceroy of Kush**. That post commanded Nubia and gave him access to its gold.

### Contradictions
- Seti II fought for a right that many of his own men doubted he deserved. The general who served him best, Setnakhte, was himself a man whose claim rested on deeds rather than blood.
- Amenmesse's case was ability, but making it meant splitting Egypt in two while the north was falling apart.

## The two crowns
The war was a geographical split between north and south.

| | **North: Seti II** | **South: Amenmesse** |
|---|---|---|
| Territory | Pi-Ramesses, the Delta, the northern garrisons | Thebes and Nubia |
| Crowned at | Pi-Ramesses | Thebes |
| Money | The capital's resources | Nubian gold |
| Religious backing | — | The priesthood of Amun |

*Texture:* Egypt's old division into Upper and Lower Egypt gave the split a natural seam. A king in Thebes could take over the southern half of the administration rather than build one from nothing.

## Course of the war
- **Stalemate.** For most of its length the war was a stalemate between the two halves of Egypt.
- **Betrayals.** The war turned on officials who changed sides, not on decisive battles.
- **Deir el-Medina.** The tomb-builders' village at Thebes was fought over and ransacked.
  - Seti II's tomb was plundered.
  - A foreman was killed by "the enemy."
- **Amenmesse's death.** Amenmesse died. How he died is not established. His death did not end the war.
- **Siptah inherits.** Siptah was a teenager when his father died. He inherited the southern crown and its army.
- **The kingmaker.** Siptah was made king by a foreign-born chancellor who could never wear the crown himself. This kingmaker:
  - carried the southern cause after Amenmesse's death;
  - arranged Siptah's marriage to **Twosret**, a Hittite princess and sister of the Hittite king **Šuppiluliuma II**. The marriage was the Hittites' last entanglement in Egyptian affairs before their own fall.
  - planned the killing of Seti II.
- **The death of Seti II.** Siptah killed his uncle, a crowned king. Seti II left no surviving heirs.
- **The fall of the kingmaker.** Once Siptah held the whole throne, he and Twosret executed the kingmaker as "the great enemy." It was Twosret who demanded his death.
- **Setnakhte's victory.** The kingmaker's clients were left without a patron. Their defection to Setnakhte let him break Siptah within the year. Siptah died in that defeat.
- **The hidden child.** Twosret was already pregnant. Her child was born in secret around 1184, and almost nobody knows it exists.

## The world outside
- The war was fought in the same years that Ugarit and the northern ports fell (c. 1192–1190).
  - Ugarit's king had sent his army north to help the Hittites, and relief reached Ugarit only after it was sacked.
  - Tyre took in the wrecked ships and their crews.
- The Hittite empire and its capital, Hattusa, have fallen by 1160. When Hattusa fell relative to Twosret's marriage is not fixed.
- Soon after the war, Egypt's new dynasty arranged Ramesses III's marriage to Isis of Tyre (c. 1185–1182) to draw northern Canaan back into its orbit.

## Setnakhte's settlement
Setnakhte took the throne around 1185 and built his legitimacy on the role of avenger.
- He gave Seti II a grand reburial.
- He executed Seti II's killers.
- He called himself Seti II's chosen heir.
- His regime carried out Egypt's own practice of erasing condemned kings:
  - Amenmesse and Siptah were struck from the king lists;
  - their statues were recarved;
  - their officials were erased.
- *Texture:* to Egyptians, erasing a name is worse than denying a funeral, because it attacks the dead person's life after death.
- **The official history** calls the war a time of foreign gold and anarchy that Setnakhte ended. It contains no rival kings.
- Setnakhte died either in battle while taking the throne or after a very short reign; which one is open.

### Contradictions in the settlement
- Setnakhte fought for inheritance by birth, then took a crown his own birth did not entitle him to.
- The man he killed, Siptah, was a direct descendant of Ramesses II. Setnakhte's own line is not.
- His legitimacy as avenger depends on a history that removes the very kings he defeated.

## What is remembered in 1160
- Temple scribes teach the official version. Only old men remember otherwise.
- Siptah's child, if alive, is a direct descendant of Ramesses II, and is therefore technically the rightful heir over Ramesses III's line.
  - That child is more than a rival claimant: their existence proves the dynasty's founding story is a lie.
- **Deir el-Medina** remembers being fought over and ransacked. That memory lies behind the strike that opens the story.
- **The Nubian gold family** has a wartime past. The southern crown was paid for with Nubian gold. Whether the family backed Amenmesse to the end or changed sides is undecided.
- **The kingmaker's family** may still be alive in 1160. Whether they are, and where they stand in the harem conspiracy, is deliberately open.

## Legacy in Ramesses III
- The war is the first run of the Aegon question: a legitimate elder son judged unfit, against a younger claimant whom men follow for his ability.
- Ramesses III's contempt for Aegon has a layer of dread beneath it.
- He has a buried sympathy for capable younger claimants. His dynasty's founding story forbids him to feel it.
- His obsession with legacy sits on top of a curated history. His own name depends on records his father had rewritten.
- The harem conspiracy repeats the pattern: a usurping claim set against an heir judged unfit.

## Divergences from the historical record
- **Relationship.** Canon makes Seti II and Amenmesse brothers, with Seti the elder. Historically their relationship is uncertain: they may have been half-brothers, or father and son.
- **Amenmesse as Viceroy of Kush.** Historically this is only a disputed scholarly theory (that he was the viceroy Messuy).
- **Seti II's death.** Historically Seti II was not killed by Siptah, and how he died is unknown.
- **Siptah.** Historically Siptah was a boy-king under regents. Here he becomes sole king by killing Seti II.
- **The kingmaker.** Historically the kingmaker was Chancellor Bay, executed in Siptah's Year 5 for unknown reasons. Here the kingmaker is unnamed, and Twosret demands his death.
- **Twosret.** Historically Twosret was Seti II's Egyptian widow and later ruled as pharaoh. Here she is Siptah's Hittite wife.
- **The erasure.** Historically Seti II, not Setnakhte, erased Amenmesse.
- **Dates.** The war is compressed from roughly 1203–1187 into 1190–1185.

## Proposed open questions
- What made Seti II seem unfit, if not vice, and was the judgment of him wrong?
- How did Amenmesse die?
- How and where did Siptah kill Seti II, and where was Setnakhte while Siptah held the whole throne?
- Did the Nubian gold family back Amenmesse to the end, or change sides, and is that why Pi-Ramesses distrusts or rewards them?
- Who outside the court still keeps the true king list: a Theban priestly house, the guild's archivists, or Nubian records?
- Does the official history tell a story about Twosret, for example that she died or was sent back to a fallen Hatti?
- Which side did the Sherden fight on, as Ramesses II's own corps?
- Did Ramesses III fight in the war or witness it?

## Hooks
- The instigators of the Deir el-Medina strike exploit an old village feud between the line of the murdered foreman and the families who profited under "the enemy."
- Courtiers whisper that Aegon is Seti II and Jon is Amenmesse. Ramesses III's buried sympathy for able younger sons colours how he treats Jon's Sherden service.
- A recarved statue at Thebes still shows traces of Amenmesse's cartouche, and a POV character learns what the scribes will not teach.
- The kingmaker's surviving family, raised under the dynasty that rose on their patron's fall, sells its services to Tiye or to the Littlefinger figure.
- The hidden heir undermines Pentawer and Aegon equally, since both descend from Setnakhte. Either side might want to use the heir or bury them.
- After the coup, Pentawer's regime uses Setnakhte's own method of erasure on Ramesses III. The king obsessed with legacy is chiselled out the same way his father chiselled out others.
- Twosret mirrors Isis: both princesses were spent by a brother on a foreign throne. Twosret's house collapsed behind her, which is exactly the fate Isis fears for Tyre.
- Old men who remember the rival kings become dangerous witnesses once rumours of a child of Siptah begin to spread.
