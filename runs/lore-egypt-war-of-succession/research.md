## Background (context)

### Antiquarian: Bronze Age grounding

#### What actually happened (the historical skeleton)
- Ramesses II outlived many of his heirs, which tangled the succession. Dodson sees this as the root of everything that followed.
- Merneptah named Seti II his successor. When Merneptah died, Seti II took the throne in the north. Seti II reigned about 5 years 10 months, c. 1203–1197 BCE.
- Amenmesse was a rival king of royal but disputed parentage. He may have been Seti II's half-brother, a son of Merneptah by Queen Takhat, or Seti's own son. His name means 'fashioned by Amun', and he took the epithet 'Ruler of Thebes'. One older view (Kitchen, von Beckerath) has him seizing the throne while the crown prince was away in Asia.
- Amenmesse held Thebes, Upper Egypt and Nubia during roughly Seti II's Years 2–4, and is attested in Theban records only for Years 3–4. Thebes fell to him within about 18 months of the rising, and he pushed north as far as the Fayyum.
- Seti II won back the whole country by his Year 5. Amenmesse then vanishes from the record, and his mummy was never found among the royal caches. Seti died in Year 6, and nobody knows whether it was natural or foul play.
- Siptah followed as a child king. Scholars disagree whether he was Seti's son or Amenmesse's; Dodson argues Amenmesse's. He ruled under the regency of Twosret, Seti's widow, and Chancellor Bay, a self-made official of Syrian descent with no royal family ties. Bay was executed in about Siptah's Year 5.
- Twosret then ruled alone. She was displaced by Setnakhte, who finished pacifying the country in his Year 2.
- From start to finish this is about 16 years of instability (c. 1203–1187). The dating is fiercely contested: Krauss and Dodson have Seti succeed Merneptah directly, with Amenmesse as a mid-reign rebel.

#### The war's map: north against south
- **North (Seti II):** the Delta capital at Pi-Ramesses, the residence and the legitimate succession. Seti also expanded the copper mining at Timna in Edom.
- **South (Amenmesse):** Thebes and the Amun priesthood. The Theban High Priest of Amun, Roma called Roy, was among the officials Seti later attacked or replaced, which marks him as an Amenmesse man.
- **Nubia was the decisive defection.** Amenmesse gained military strength through Khaemtjitry, the King's Son of Kush (Viceroy of Nubia), who went over to him. The viceroy commanded Nubia's garrisons, revenues and gold. Khaemtjitry then served Amenmesse as vizier.
- Dodson and Krauss go further and suggest that Amenmesse was himself a former Viceroy of Kush named Messuy. Yurco objects that Messuy's monuments were never defaced, which they would have been if he were the rebel.
- Egypt had two viziers, one for the south and one for the north. A rival king in Thebes could therefore take over a complete southern administration rather than build one from scratch. Amenmesse appointed and dismissed southern viziers himself.
- Amenmesse built at Karnak. At least six quartzite statues in the hypostyle hall were probably his, later recut with Seti II's name.

#### The war at Deir el-Medina (the tomb-builders' village)
- The crew building the royal tombs at Thebes worked for whichever king held Thebes. Seti II's tomb (KV15) was ransacked or vandalized during the rivalry and repaired afterward. Amenmesse's own tomb (KV10) was later hacked clean of his name.
- Papyrus Salt 124 is an indictment brought by the workman Amennakht against Paneb, a chief workman.
 - Neferhotep, Amennakht's brother and Paneb's adoptive father, was a chief workman who was killed by 'the enemy'. Scholars read that enemy as either foreigners or a domestic army during Amenmesse's hold on Thebes.
 - Paneb had once chased Neferhotep through the village threatening to 'kill him in the night' and beat nine men in a single night.
 - When the vizier Amenmose tried to punish Paneb, Paneb appealed over his head to 'Mose' (very likely Amenmesse), and the vizier was dismissed.
 - Paneb is also accused of stealing stone from Seti II's tomb and of bribing the vizier for Neferhotep's post.
 - One later dating puts the papyrus in Siptah's Year 5 instead.
- In a civil war, then, the village's internal feuds became a matter of which king you could appeal to.

#### What the winners did: erasure and the language of 'the enemy'
- Seti II carried out a damnatio memoriae on Amenmesse. His agents erased nearly all of Amenmesse's texts and scenes in KV10. Officials of the rebel regime were attacked or replaced, and Khaemtjitry's images were erased even from the years before the rising. Seti then referred to Amenmesse as 'the enemy'.
- An ostracon records that Pharaoh 'has killed the great enemy, Bay', so even a royal chancellor could be named an enemy of the state and executed.
- Setnakhte took over KV14, the tomb that had been cut for Twosret.
- Seti II's tomb was still unfinished when he died, and his successor buried him in it.

#### Setnakhte's official version of the war
- Ramesses III's own retrospective survives in Papyrus Harris I, written c. 1152 under Ramesses IV. In Breasted's translation, 'the land of Egypt was overthrown from without, and every man was thrown out of his right'.
 - A Syrian (or Shasu) chief called Irsu ('he who made himself') or Su 'set the whole land tributary before him'.
 - 'They made the gods like men, and no offerings were presented in the temples'.
 - Setnakhte 'set in order the entire land which had been rebellious; he slew the rebels'.
- Setnakhte's Elephantine stela, dated Year 2, II Shemu 10, says his opponents had hired Asiatic reinforcements with silver and gold and then fled, leaving the treasure behind, 'like sparrows with a falcon after them'. It likens him to the god Seth, who acts 'to remove from Egypt those who led it astray'.
- Who these enemies were is unclear. Bay cannot be Irsu, because Bay was already dead. Goedicke places Irsu in Canaan, while Dodson and Servajean read the events as inside Egypt. Van Cleve (2024) argues there was no Twosret–Setnakhte war at all, and that Setnakhte recycled Twosret's victory over Bay.
- Setnakhte's own origin is unknown. He was probably a minor descendant of Ramesses II. His wife Tiy-Merenese may have been a daughter of Merneptah.

#### Where canon diverges from this record
- **Who fights whom.** Canon's Seti II and Amenmesse are brothers, with Seti II the elder. Historically their relationship is unknown: they may be half-brothers, or father and son.
- **Siptah.** Historically Siptah was a child king run by regents. In canon he is a grown rival pharaoh who kills Seti II. Historically, how Seti died is unknown.
- **Twosret.** Historically she was Seti II's Egyptian widow and later ruled as pharaoh. In canon she is a Hittite princess married to Siptah.
- **Dates.** Canon compresses about 16 years (c. 1203–1187) into 1190–1185 and moves it later. That puts it in the same years as the fall of Ugarit (canon c. 1192–1190) and the collapse of Hatti, so any Hittite help for Siptah would come from a crumbling empire.
- **Ramesses III's bloodline.** If Tiy-Merenese really was a daughter of Merneptah, Ramesses III would be a royal grandson through his mother. Canon makes his royal blood distant.

### Psychologist: character friction

#### Frame: what canon fixes, and the ages it implies
The local source folder is empty, so everything below comes from the canon in the brief and from general historical knowledge. Nothing here is cited.

What canon fixes:
- Two grandsons of Ramesses II, the elder **Seti II** and the younger **Amenmesse**, fought over the throne from 1190 to 1185. Canon calls this "two brothers plus a nephew", so both are sons of the unnamed king who reigned 1210–~1190.
- Amenmesse died. His son **Siptah** took up the claim and killed his uncle Seti II, who left no surviving heirs.
- Siptah's wife **Twosret** was a Hittite princess and the sister of Šuppiluliuma II.
- **Setnakhte**, Seti II's top general, defeated Siptah. Siptah died in that defeat. Twosret was already pregnant, and the child was born in secret around 1184.

Ages worked out from canon dates. These are estimates, not settled:
- **Ramesses II** lives from about 1300 to 1210, so he dies around 90. His heir waits under him for decades and comes to the throne old, probably in his 50s.
- **Seti II and Amenmesse** were probably in their 40s or 50s by 1190. They were grown men with households and factions, not boys.
- **Siptah** has to be an adult by 1185, old enough to kill his uncle and father a child.
- **Setnakhte** is "a second son of a second son of a third daughter" of Ramesses II, which puts his birth around 1240–1250. He was a seasoned general in his 50s.
- **Ramesses III**'s age is open. But Aegon is in his late teens or early 20s in 1160, which means Ramesses III was at least an adolescent during the war and quite possibly a young officer in his father's army.
- **Ugarit** falls around 1192–1190, just as the civil war begins. Egypt is tearing itself apart while the north burns.

#### Historical analogue (real record; canon diverges)
- **Merneptah**: historically the 13th son of Ramesses II, about 60 when he took the throne, and he reigned about 10 years. Canon gives the unnamed son about 20 years.
- **Seti II**: Merneptah's crown prince. **Amenmesse** ruled Upper Egypt and Nubia as a rival for a few years. Scholars debate whether Amenmesse was Seti's brother or son, and whether he was the former Viceroy of Kush, Messuy. That last identification is speculative. Seti II later erased Amenmesse's names from monuments.
- **Siptah**: historically a boy-king of uncertain parentage, sometimes argued to be Amenmesse's son. His mummy shows a deformed foot and he died at about 16. He was steered by the Syrian-born chancellor **Bay**, a foreign kingmaker, whom he had executed in his Year 5. *Divergence: canon makes Siptah an adult who kills Seti II.*
- **Twosret**: historically Seti II's Egyptian Great Royal Wife. She was regent for Siptah and then ruled alone. *Divergence: canon makes her Siptah's Hittite wife.*
- **Setnakhte**: his Elephantine stela describes chaos and rebels who hired 'Asiatics' with temple gold. Papyrus Harris I remembers 'empty years' and a Syrian upstart named 'Irsu', possibly Bay.
- **Deir el-Medina**: the Theban tomb workers lived under whichever claimant held Thebes. The scandals around the foreman Paneb, recorded in Papyrus Salt 124, date to this period.
- **Hittite precedent**: Hittite royal history is a long run of kin-killing usurpations. The Telipinu Proclamation was written to stop them, and Hattusili III later deposed his nephew Urhi-Teshub. A Hittite princess would grow up hearing these stories.

#### The old king (Ramesses II's heir, name unset)
- **Want:** to be king in his own right after decades as the old giant's son. **Need:** to settle the succession before he died. **Lie:** "There is time."
- **Competing obligations:** custom says the elder son inherits, but the realm needs the abler one, and he also loves whichever son is easier to love. He cannot satisfy all three.
- **Pressure point:** he waited so long under Ramesses II that he may never have truly believed in his own death. A man who reaches the throne at 55 can postpone the hardest decision until it is made for him.
- **Worst act (by omission):** he let two grown sons build rival households and factions, then died and left them to fight it out.

#### Seti II, the elder
- **Want:** the throne owed to him by birth. **Need:** to deserve it in the eyes of the men who would die for him. **Lie:** "Being born first is itself a virtue."
- **Competing obligations:** to his father's order, to the brother he grew up with, and to his own sons. Canon says he left no surviving heir, so dead sons may be part of who he is.
- **What would break him:** proof that his own general (Setnakhte) or the old court preferred his brother. **What would turn him:** almost nothing. Legitimacy is the only thing he has.
- **Justifying the worst act:** if he ordered his brother's name or line erased, the reasoning was simple: "A rival king is not a brother. Ma'at allows one Horus."

#### Amenmesse, the younger
- **Want:** the throne. **Need:** his father's recognition, which he never fully had. **Lie:** "I take what Egypt needs, not what I want."
- **Competing obligations:** loyalty to his brother against the conviction that the order of birth chose wrong. He also owes something to his son Siptah, to whom he passes the claim along with the grudge.
- **What would break him:** being ignored rather than defeated. Being treated as a rebel instead of a rival.
- **Justifying the worst act:** splitting Egypt in two in the middle of the Collapse: "If the elder will not hold it, someone must."

#### Siptah, the son who inherited the grudge
- **Want:** to finish his father's war. **Need:** a self that is not just his father's claim. **Lie:** "I avenged a wrong." In fact he killed his uncle, a crowned king.
- **Competing obligations:** to his dead father, to the Hittite alliance his marriage stands for, and to the wife and unborn child who will pay for whatever he does.
- **What would break him:** discovering that his foreign backer was collapsing and his allies were waiting to see who won.
- **Justifying the worst act:** the kinslaying of Seti II. "He destroyed my father's name; I only answered."
- *Divergence from history:* canon makes Siptah an adult kinslayer, not a lame boy-king.

#### Twosret, the Hittite bride
- **Want:** at first, to be queen of the winning side, which was her brother's bet. Later, revenge, or nothing at all. **Need:** for the child to live. **Lie:** possibly "I am protecting the child," when part of her is also punishing herself or erasing her own failure.
- **Competing obligations:** to Hatti, which sent her; to Siptah's claim; and to the child, who can only survive without that claim. She cannot honour all three.
- **Her situation:** both her homes were dying at once. Hattusa was falling behind her and her husband died in front of her. Like Isis, she was exported by her brother. Unlike Isis, the house behind her collapsed.
- **Her fate is open.** Nothing here settles it, or who hid the child.

#### Setnakhte, the avenger who became king
- **Want:** to avenge Seti II and restore order. **Need:** to admit that he took the throne. **Lie:** "I did not seize the crown; I picked it up off the floor."
- **Competing obligations:** the principle of birth-right he fought for, against a realm with no legitimate heir left, against his own son's future.
- **Pressure point:** any rumour of a living child of Siptah. That would turn his vengeance into usurpation in his own eyes.
- **Justifying the worst act:** killing Siptah, a direct descendant of Ramesses II: "He murdered a king. I am the king's justice, not his rival."
- How Setnakhte died remains open.

#### Ramesses III, the war's inheritor
- He watched Egypt split while Ugarit and Hatti fell.
- He inherited a crown taken by a man who had fought to defend inheritance by birth. That is why distance in blood matters so much to him, and why he idolizes Ramesses II: what he lacks by birth he must earn by deeds.
- **Pressure point:** an unfit eldest son of his own. In Aegon he sees the war's opening position being set up again.

#### Friction map
- **Seti II ↔ Amenmesse:** the elder's right against the younger's ability. Each thinks the other is the usurper.
- **The old king ↔ both sons:** whatever love he showed, he left the decision unmade. Both sons can claim his favour.
- **Siptah ↔ Seti II:** a nephew kills his uncle. This is the act that makes Setnakhte an avenger rather than just a soldier.
- **Setnakhte ↔ Seti II:** a servant loyal to the principle more than the man. Did he love his master, or only his master's claim?
- **Setnakhte ↔ Siptah:** the avenger kills the last of Ramesses II's direct male line and becomes the thing he punished.
- **Twosret ↔ Šuppiluliuma II:** a brother who spent his sister on a losing bet. This mirrors Isis and her brother.
- **Twosret ↔ Setnakhte:** the widow has to hide from the victor. Her child is the one fact that could undo his self-justification.
- **Twosret ↔ Isis:** both are foreign princesses married into Egypt in the same years. One grabs rank; the other may have buried it. Whether they ever met is open.
- **Ramesses III ↔ his father's ghost:** pride and shame in a single debt.
- **Old court families ↔ the 20th Dynasty:** houses that backed Amenmesse or Siptah survived under the men who beat them. This is a tension, not a plot.

### Historian: historical analogues

#### The real record behind the names (and where canon diverges)
- **Who Amenmesse was.** His parentage is disputed. He may have been a son of Merneptah and Queen Takhat, or a son of Ramesses II. His epithet meant "Ruler of Thebes". Some scholars think he seized the throne from the crown prince Seti. Others argue that Seti II succeeded Merneptah directly and that Amenmesse was a rival king ruling in parallel.
- **Where he ruled.** His authority is attested only in Upper Egypt (Thebes, in his Years 3–4) and in Nubia. Seti II's own Years 3–4 are unaccounted for, which supports a north-against-south split.
- **The Messuy theory.** Krauss and Dodson suggest Amenmesse was once Messuy, the Viceroy of Kush. That would mean he launched his bid from command of Nubia and its gold. Yurco disputes it. Messuy's successor as viceroy, Khaemtir, apparently changed sides and became Amenmesse's vizier.
- **Seti II's revenge.** After he won, Seti II's agents erased or overwrote nearly every text in Amenmesse's tomb (KV10). They recarved his Karnak statues with Seti's name and attacked his officials, including the High Priest of Amun Roy and Khaemtir, whose Nubian career was erased so thoroughly that it was rediscovered only in the 1970s. Amenmesse's mummy has never been found. Seti's own Theban tomb had earlier been ransacked under Amenmesse, and Seti's officials later restored it.
- **The tomb-builders' village took sides.** In Papyrus Salt 124, the workman Amennakht accuses the foreman Paneb of crimes. He says the previous foreman, Neferhotep, was killed by "the enemy" under "Msy", which is probably Amenmesse. That phrase may mean an army backing Amenmesse. Paneb allegedly bought his post by giving the vizier five of the dead man's servants. When one vizier tried to discipline him, Paneb appealed straight to Amenmesse and had that vizier dismissed. He is also accused of stripping Seti II's tomb and its worksite. The papyrus's date and reliability are disputed.
- **The kingmaker.** Chancellor Bay, a man of foreign (Syrian) origin, claimed in inscriptions that he "established the king on the seat of his father", meaning Siptah. Ostracon IFAO 1864 announced to the Deir el-Medina workmen that Bay, "the great enemy", had been executed on royal order in Siptah's Year 5. A damaged statue in Munich may make Siptah Amenmesse's son.
- **Setnakhte's version.** Setnakhte's Elephantine stele says he expelled rebels who fled leaving behind gold, silver and copper stolen from Egypt, wealth they had meant to use to hire Asiatic reinforcements. Resistance ended only in his Year 2. The Great Harris Papyrus later painted the time before him as anarchy under "Irsu, a Syrian" who made himself chief.
- **Divergence (canon wins).** Historically, Seti II was not killed by a nephew, and Siptah reigned under Bay's guardianship. Twosret was Seti II's Egyptian queen, and she ruled after Siptah. Canon instead has Amenmesse die in the war, his son Siptah kill Seti II, and Setnakhte avenge Seti by defeating Siptah.
- **Fit with canon.** The Munich statue reading and the parallel-reign model both fit canon's account of two brothers plus a nephew.

#### Analogue: the Mughal War of Succession, 1657–1659
- **What happened.** Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657. His eldest son, Dara Shikoh, already acted as regent at court, and his brothers resented it. The other three sons each governed a great province with its own army and revenue: Shuja held Bengal, Aurangzeb the Deccan, and Murad Gujarat.
- **How it worked.** Shuja and Murad each proclaimed themselves emperor. Dara beat Shuja near Benares. Aurangzeb promised to back Murad, and their combined army broke Dara at Samugarh in 1658. Aurangzeb then had Murad arrested by treachery and imprisoned, crowned himself, and destroyed Shuja at Khajwa in 1659.
- **What drove the actors.** The empire had no fixed rule of succession. Whoever held the capital and the father's person had legitimacy. The frontier governors had the veteran armies and the money. Each brother knew that losing meant death or prison.
- **Why it ended as it did.** The patient brother with the hardest frontier army won. He used one sibling to destroy another and then discarded him.
- **What transfers.** A provincial command, such as the Viceroy of Kush, works as a ready-made war chest and army. The heir at court holds the capital but not the veterans. Alliances between brothers are tactical and are betrayed the moment they have served.

#### Analogue: the Ottoman Interregnum, 1402–1413
- **What happened.** Timur captured Sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402. His sons then fought an eleven-year civil war across Anatolia and the Balkans.
- **How it worked.** The realm split along geography. Süleyman held the European provinces (Rumelia) and Mehmed held the east. Süleyman first took Bursa and Ankara. After a five-year stalemate, Mehmed sent his brother Musa into Europe, and the two allied against Süleyman, who was strangled in 1411. Mehmed then defeated and killed Musa in 1413 and ruled alone. A further brother, Mustafa, surfaced later as a claimant and asked to split the empire. Mehmed refused.
- **What drove the actors.** The dynasty's prestige survived, but there was no central authority. Each brother's power was his region's army and its local notables, and outside powers backed whichever claimant suited them.
- **What transfers.** A war of succession can drag through years of stalemate, broken by a proxy. The last survivor is not always the original claimant. And a 'dead' claimant can reappear years later, as Mustafa did.

#### Analogue: kingmakers barred from the throne (Ricimer; Bay)
- **Ricimer.** In the 5th-century Western Roman Empire, the general Ricimer was of barbarian birth and an Arian Christian, so he could not be recognized as emperor himself. He ruled through puppets instead. He raised Majorian and then deposed him, and he set up Libius Severus, Anthemius and Olybrius in turn.
- **How it worked.** The kingmaker controls the army and the treasury but lacks the blood or the creed for the crown, so he needs a young or weak claimant with the right name. The claimant needs him until he does not.
- **Bay as the Egyptian version.** Bay was a foreign-born chancellor who boasted of setting Siptah on his father's seat and was executed as "the great enemy" in the king's fifth year. The ostracon gives no motive. Some sources credit Twosret with the move, while others say Siptah acted on his own or that Bay overreached.
- **What transfers.** A kingmaker's power is real but borrowed. The puppet, or the puppet's wife, eventually has every reason to remove him, and the kingmaker's own clients become free agents when he falls.

#### Analogue: the avenger who founds a dynasty (Septimius Severus)
- **What happened.** In 193 CE the Praetorians murdered the emperor Pertinax and sold the throne. Septimius Severus, who had served under Pertinax, marched on Rome declaring himself Pertinax's avenger.
- **How it worked.** He executed the soldiers who had killed Pertinax, took Pertinax's name, and had him deified. He later declared himself the adopted son of Marcus Aurelius, which grafted his new house onto the old dynasty.
- **What drove him.** He was a provincial general with no dynastic claim. Vengeance for a legitimate master turned a usurpation into a restoration.
- **What transfers.** A general from outside the dynasty legitimizes himself as the dead king's avenger and heir. He honours the murdered king, kills or erases the murderers, and rewrites the record so that his house appears to continue the old line. This is very close to canon's Setnakhte, and it matches the real Setnakhte's stele and the Harris Papyrus.

#### Analogue: Hittite royals entangled in Egyptian succession
- **The Zannanza affair (14th century BCE).** A widowed Egyptian queen, "Dakhamunzu", wrote to Šuppiluliuma I asking for a Hittite son to marry and make king. The prince Zannanza was killed, probably on the road, and a later Hittite prayer says outright that hostile Egyptians killed him. Angry letters followed between Hatti and the next pharaoh, Ay.
- **Urhi-Teshub (13th century BCE).** The deposed Hittite king Urhi-Teshub (Muršili III) fled to Egypt. Hattusili III demanded his extradition, Ramesses II claimed not to know where he was, and the two empires came close to war. The peace treaty that followed included an extradition clause.
- **What transfers.** Egypt's factions have long treated a Hittite royal at the Egyptian throne as a mortal threat. Egypt has also, at least once, quietly sheltered a fallen Hittite royal against Hatti's demands.

### Mythographer: fictional analogues

#### Source material: what the record suggests (history, not fiction)
This is real history. Canon has changed it, and the divergences are labeled below.

- **Amenmesse's base was the south.** Some scholars argue that he ruled Upper Egypt as a rival king during Seti II's poorly attested third and fourth years. Others argue he never reigned between Merneptah and Seti II.
- **A possible Nubian link.** One theory makes Amenmesse the former viceroy of Kush, Messuy. Yurco rejected it, partly because Messuy's images in Nubia were never defaced.
- **The war was waged with chisels too.** Seti II's agents erased almost every text and scene in Amenmesse's tomb (KV10). The scenes of the viceroy Khaemtir were wiped so thoroughly that his viceroyalty was nearly unknown until the 1970s. Seti II also re-carved Amenmesse's names with his own.
- **The victor's account.** Setnakhte's Elephantine stela says resistance ended only in his Year 2. It tells of 'Asiatic' rebels who fled, leaving looted gold, silver and copper behind. Papyrus Harris I, written later under the Ramessides, says the land fell to chiefs and town rulers in 'empty years'. It names a Syrian, Irsu ('he who made himself'), among those chiefs. The chancellor Bay was once identified as Irsu, but that identification has largely collapsed.
- **Divergence:** in canon, Seti II and Amenmesse are brothers. Amenmesse dies, his son Siptah kills Seti II, and Setnakhte, Seti II's general, defeats Siptah. Twosret is a Hittite princess married to Siptah. Historically, the family relationships are uncertain and Twosret was Seti II's Egyptian queen.

#### Fiction: Shakespeare's Henriad (Richard II through Henry V)
**What happens.** Bolingbroke deposes Richard II and becomes Henry IV. He spends his reign haunted by how he got the crown. On his deathbed he advises his son to 'busy giddy minds with foreign quarrels'. As Henry V, the son prays on the eve of Agincourt: 'think not upon the fault my father made in compassing the crown.' He lists his penances, among them reburying Richard and paying for prayers.

**What the device does.** A usurpation becomes an inheritance. The son did not commit the crime, but he lives as if he owes for it, and a foreign war becomes both his proof and his penance.

**Why it works.** Readers feel guilt passing between generations as dread: every victory might be the one the gods refuse.

**Pitfalls.** The legitimacy-anxious king who just wants glory is a stock figure. The father's guilt can also become a speech instead of a pressure on what the son decides. Agincourt-style vindication is a cliché to subvert, not to repeat.

**Bronze Age transformation.** Egyptian kingship already runs on maat (cosmic order) restored, on the cult of royal ancestors, and on deeds carved in stone. Penance would take the form of offerings for the dead, temple building and a war carved in stone. It would not take the form of prayer or chantries.

#### Myth and drama: Eteocles and Polyneices (Seven Against Thebes, Antigone)
**What happens.** Oedipus's sons agree to rule Thebes in alternate years. One refuses to hand over power. The other brings a foreign army (the Seven) against his own city, and the brothers kill each other in single combat. Their uncle Creon inherits. His first act as king is a decree on the dead: one brother is buried with honour, and the other is left to rot as a traitor.

**What it does.** A shared throne is a fuse. Whoever breaks the deal makes the other into an invader. The war is finally settled by a third man who was not in the bloodline's front rank.

**Why it works.** Both brothers can claim to be in the right. The real horror falls on the survivors, who must live under the winner's account of who was loyal and who was a traitor.

**Pitfalls.** An inherited curse can make the outcome feel fated, so that nobody chooses anything. Symmetrical brothers also blur together; they need distinct motives.

**Bronze Age fit.** Egypt already had a natural seam for a divided kingdom, Upper and Lower Egypt. For Egyptians, denying a burial or erasing a name is worse than denying a funeral: it destroys a person's life after death.

#### Fiction: the Dance of the Dragons (GRRM, Fire & Blood)
**What happens.** Half-siblings Rhaenyra and Aegon II fight over their father's throne. The Greens crown Aegon II first, while Rhaenyra is away. Both claimants die. Rhaenyra's son becomes king, and the dragons, the realm's ultimate weapon, are nearly wiped out.

**What the device does.** It is backstory written as a disputed chronicle. In-world sources contradict each other, and the war's real legacy is what it destroyed rather than who won.

**Why it works.** An unreliable history lets later characters argue about the past. It also lets the author reveal truths about it without retconning anything.

**Pitfalls.** It invites a long catalogue of battles. Faction colours (Greens and Blacks) also flatten people into teams. Fans of the source will notice a direct port.

**Bronze Age transformation.** Leave out the chronicler. Egypt kept its history on monuments: stelae, king lists and re-carved cartouches. The 'disputed sources' would be stones that contradict each other, some of them chiselled.

#### Myth: Osiris, Set and the hidden Horus
**What happens.** Set murders his brother Osiris. The widow hides the infant Horus, the rightful heir, in the papyrus marshes of the Delta at Chemmis. There he survives scorpions and snakes until he is strong enough to challenge his uncle.

**What it does.** This is the founding Egyptian myth of a stolen throne and a posthumous heir in hiding. Every pharaoh claimed to be Horus.

**Why it works on an Egyptian audience.** Any hidden heir automatically reads as Horus, so in-world the myth could be used either to legitimize such an heir or to cast them as a threat.

**Pitfalls.** It makes the heir's eventual triumph feel destined.

**Caution:** the widow-mother in this myth shares a name with the placeholder 'Isis'. That overlap is a coincidence and should not be built on.

### The Two Crowns: Delta king against Theban-Nubian king (Historian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: War of Succession, Seti II, Amenmesse, Siptah, Nubian nobleman, Deir el-Medina, Thebes, Pi-Ramesses

**Plot**: Make the War of Succession a geographical split on the Mughal and Ottoman model. Seti II, the elder, holds Pi-Ramesses, the Delta and the northern garrisons. Amenmesse, the younger, was Viceroy of Kush when their father died, and he crowns himself at Thebes. He pays for the war with Nubian gold and is backed by the priesthood of Amun. The war is mostly a stalemate between the two halves. It turns on betrayals: officials who change sides, and the Theban tomb-builders' village fought over and ransacked. Seti's tomb is plundered and a foreman is killed by "the enemy". Amenmesse's death does not end it. His teenage son Siptah inherits the southern crown and its army, as Mustafa reappeared after the Ottoman Interregnum seemed over.

**Integration**: This fits canon's two brothers plus a nephew and the five-year span 1190–1185, and it follows the historical evidence that Amenmesse ruled only in Thebes and Nubia (labelled divergence: historically Seti II was not killed by Siptah). It gives the Nubian gold family a wartime past, since they either bankrolled the losing southern crown or changed sides at the right moment, and it gives the Deir el-Medina strike a memory of the war behind it.

Questions this raises:
- Did the Nubian gold family back Amenmesse to the end, or change sides like Khaemtir? Is that why Pi-Ramesses still distrusts them? (unanswered)
- Are there families in the Deir el-Medina workforce whose feud dates from the war, the line of the murdered foreman against the line of the man who bought his post? (unanswered)

### Seti II and Amenmesse: the first run of the Aegon question (Psychologist)
Status: ACCEPTED WITH CHANGES by the author (settled once the author's changes below are applied)
Affects: Seti II, Amenmesse, Ramesses III, Aegon, Pentawer, Tiye

**Characterization**: Seti II is the legitimate elder and hollow: indecisive or indolent, sure that being born first is a virtue in itself. Amenmesse is the abler younger brother, convinced that the order of birth was an accident of the gods. He tells himself he takes what Egypt needs, not what he wants. Setnakhte chose the unfit legitimate son, and good men died for him. Make Seti II's failing different from Aegon's vices, for example timidity or grief over dead sons, so that the echo is in the structure, not a copy. It gives Ramesses III's contempt for Aegon a layer of dread underneath. He also has a buried sympathy for capable younger claimants that his own dynasty's founding story forbids.

### Setnakhte the Avenger and the erased kings (Historian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Setnakhte, Ramesses III, Hidden heir, Amenmesse, Siptah, Seti II, Egyptian priesthood

**World**: Ramesses III's legitimacy rests on a Severan founding story. Setnakhte took the throne as the avenger of Seti II. He gave Seti a grand reburial, executed Seti's killers, and called himself Seti's chosen heir. Following Egypt's own practice of erasing condemned kings, his regime struck Amenmesse and Siptah from the king lists, recarved their statues and erased their officials. Official history calls the war a time of foreign gold and anarchy that Setnakhte ended, with no rival kings in it. In 1160, temple scribes teach this version and only old men remember otherwise. That makes a living child of Siptah more than a rival claimant: proof that the dynasty's founding story is a lie.

**Integration**: This builds directly on canon's Setnakhte avenging Seti II and his distant royal blood, and it raises the stakes of the hidden heir without deciding who the heir is. It also sits well with Ramesses III's obsession with legacy, since his own name depends on a curated history.

Questions this raises:
- Who outside the court still keeps the true king list: a Theban priestly house, the guild's archivists, or Nubian records? (unanswered)
- Does Setnakhte's avenger claim also cover a Twosret story, for example that she died or was sent back to a fallen Hatti? (unanswered)

### Siptah's Kingmaker (Historian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Siptah, Twosret, Setnakhte, Seti II, War of Succession

**Plot**: Siptah was a teenager when his father died, and he was made king by a Bay figure: a foreign-born chancellor who could never wear the crown himself, as Ricimer could not. This kingmaker carried the southern cause after Amenmesse's death, arranged the Hittite marriage to Twosret, and planned the killing of Seti II. Once Siptah held the whole throne, he and Twosret executed the kingmaker as "the great enemy". The kingmaker's clients were left without a patron, and their defection to Setnakhte, the way Aurangzeb discarded Murad and Murad's men changed sides, is what let Setnakhte break Siptah within the year.

**Integration**: Canon already has Siptah kill Seti II and fall to Setnakhte. This supplies the mechanism of that fall from the real record, where Bay was executed in Siptah's Year 5 for unknown reasons. Nothing settled about the hidden heir changes.

Questions this raises:
- Is the kingmaker's family still alive in 1160, and on which side of the harem conspiracy? -> Author's answer: leave this open. it's an enticing idea
- Was Twosret the one who demanded his death, as some scholars suspect of the historical Tausret? -> Author's answer: yes
