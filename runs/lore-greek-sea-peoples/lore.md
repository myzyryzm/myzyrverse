# The Greek Sea Peoples

In this world the Sea Peoples are the Mycenaean Greeks. They were never one kingdom. They are warlords who fight one another and make brief "enemy of my enemy" truces: pirate wildlings. They are not outsiders who wrecked Greece. They are what remains of the Greek palaces' own sea-power, cut loose when the Drought broke the system that fed it.

This document covers how that happened and where things stand in 1160 BCE.

## The palaces and their fleets

- The Mycenaean palaces were built in Cyclopean masonry. They were originally the homes and neighbourhoods of the Cyclopes, until men drove the Cyclopes out and took them.
- Each palace was ruled by a wanax (king) and ran on rations:
  - it fed its dependants from its stores;
  - it paid its rowers and coastguards in grain and bronze.
- Raiding was already part of what palaces did. Their fleets struck Anatolian and Cypriot coasts for goods and captive women.
- The palace economy took people as plunder long before the collapse. The raiders did not invent the trade; they inherited it.

## How the palaces broke

- **The squeeze.** In their last years each wanax called up rowers and posted coastguards along the shore. The Drought cut the grain, and trade shrank under drought and raiding together. The ration system could no longer pay for the fleet.
- **The crews kept the ships.** When grain and bronze stopped coming, the crews went on doing what the palaces had always done on the side, but now for themselves.
- **The fires.** By 1160 the palace fires are about forty years in the past. Most palaces are destroyed, and a few still stand.

## The ship-lords

- **Who leads.** Leadership passed to small men:
  - local headmen (qa-si-re-u), who had been officials under the palace, never kings;
  - ship-captains.
- **Rank is counted in hulls and oars.** A galley of fifty oars is a warband of fifty.
- **Every lord is a king of something smaller.** Each holds a piece of ruined palace and calls himself king. The claim rests on a throne he never held, in a system that no longer exists.
- **A warrior caste.** By 1160 the Naue II sword marks a warrior caste.
- **Rivalry.** The lords fight one another as readily as they fight anyone else, and their alliances last only as long as a shared enemy does.

## The cistern kings and the bands outside the walls

The Sea Peoples are two groups that need each other and hate each other.

- **The cistern kings.**
  - The few palaces still standing survive because their lords walled in their water. Mycenae, Tiryns and Athens all brought their springs or cisterns inside their walls during the Drought.
  - The knowledge for these water-works came from the Cyclopes. Any further links between the cistern kings and the Cyclopes are not yet settled.
  - The cistern kings rule by controlling water:
    - they let it out to client villages;
    - they keep young warbands loyal with drink and plunder;
    - they drive out anyone they cannot feed.
- **The bands outside.** Everyone outside the walls becomes a raider:
  - retainers the kings have cast out;
  - starving herders;
  - villages fleeing the Cyclopes, whom the Drought is driving down from their caves.
- **The relationship.** The landless bands serve the cistern kings, raid them, and dream of taking their walls.

## The southern wars and the bad homecomings

Greek oral tradition counts the failed southern ventures among its great wars. Troy also exists in this world. These ventures were:

- **the invasions of Egypt**, ending in Ramesses III's victory over the Sea Peoples in his Year 8 (about 1177, some 17 years before the story).

The survivors sailed home and found their palaces burned or seized by other men and their granaries empty. Turned away, they became ship-lords with no land. Their sons raid because their fathers could not go home.

The raiders' bards sing of the Delta the way Odysseus lies about it: plunder first, then Pharaoh's terrible counterstroke. Ramesses III is the villain of their poems, just as they are the bound captives on his temple walls.

## Refugees

- **The coasts empty.** Coastal villagers have moved uphill into fortified peak villages, such as Karphi and Kastri on Crete, and the upland refuge villages of Mount Ida.
- **The landscape.** Greek and island country reads as empty shores and crowded hilltops.
- **Distrust.** Refuge communities distrust any armed band that arrives from the sea.

## Cyclopes, griffins

- **Cyclopes.** The Cyclopes leaving their caves are a cause of unrest in Greece and one source of the raiding bands. Long before the palaces, men and their griffins fought an ancient war against the Cyclopes, and human legend still remembers it.

## How Egypt sees them

- **Through war.** Egypt knows the Greek raiders as the northern invaders Ramesses III threw back at great cost, a war the nobility still resents paying for.
- **Through the Sherden.** The Sherden have no connection to the Greek Sea Peoples:
  - The original Sherden came from Sardinia. The Greeks are a different people, and the two are seen as separate groups.
  - The modern Sherden keep no connection overseas.
  - Ordinary Egyptians nonetheless lump the Sherden and the Greek raiders together as the same "foreign invader" type.

## Divergences from the historical record

- **The Sea Peoples.** Historically they were a mixed coalition, and no destruction in the Aegean can be firmly pinned on them. Here they are deliberately the Mycenaean palaces' own remnants.
- **The Sherden.** Historically they appear in Egyptian lists alongside the other Sea Peoples, and their origin is uncertain (possibly Sardinian). Here they are Sardinian and wholly unconnected to the Greek raiders, though Egyptians lump them together.
- **The Delta epic.** There is no evidence of a Greek epic tradition about invading Egypt. It is invented for this world, alongside Troy.
- **The Drought.** Here the Drought is the driving cause of the collapse. Historically drought was one factor among many, and the evidence for it in the Aegean is thin.
- **Dates.** Collapse dates follow the in-world chronology and may shift by a few years.

## Canon conflicts

- **Sherden origin.** The prehistory file factions.md says Ramesses II "founded them by recruiting Sea Peoples", and the same file defines the Sea Peoples as the Mycenaean Greeks. Read together, that makes the Sherden Greek. The author's later answers and notes say the Sherden are Sardinian and have no connection to the Greek Sea Peoples. This document follows the author's notes. Options for revising factions.md:
  - **(a)** Treat "Sea Peoples" as an Egyptian catch-all for seaborne foreigners. Ramesses II recruited Sardinian sea-raiders, whom Egyptians filed under the same label as the Greeks. This matches the author's "same foreign-invader archetype" note.
  - **(b)** Keep "Sea Peoples" strictly for the Greeks, and change the Sherden entry to "Ramesses II founded them by recruiting Sardinian warriors."
  - **(c)** Keep the word "Sea Peoples" only as the Egyptian popular name, with a note that the Sherden and the Greeks are unrelated peoples.

## Proposed open questions

- Does any warlord still claim the old title wanax, or has "king" sunk to the headman's word?
- Which citadels still stand in 1160, and is Mycenae one of them?
- Who buys captives in 1160: Cyprus, Tyre, Egypt, or all three?
- How do Troy and the southern wars relate in Greek memory: one cycle of songs, or separate ages?
- After the Year 8 victory, did Ramesses III kill, enslave or settle the captured Greeks, and are any of them living in Egypt now?
- Beyond the water-works, what other ties bind the cistern kings to the Cyclopes?
- How do Greek raiders see the Sherden: as fellow foreigners in Pharaoh's pay, or as strangers with no meaning to them?
- Did Greeks who served Hatti as mercenaries come home after Hattusa fell, or do some remain in Anatolia?

## Hooks

- On Jon's road, refuge villages that fear anything arriving from the sea see a band in Sherden horned helmets and bar their gates. His first fight in Greece is to be let in.
- Jon hears his father sung as the villain of the Delta war, perhaps in the same season he learns, or will learn, that Ramesses III is dead.
- The Cyclopes taught the water-works. As the Drought drives them down, they come back for the springs.
- Jon's exodus inverts Year 8: the son of the pharaoh who threw the Greeks back leads Greek refugees to shelter in Egypt.
- At Pi-Ramesses, the habit of lumping the Sherden with the raiders becomes a political weapon. The nobles or Tiye's faction use it to make the guard look foreign at the moment of the coup.
- The captive trade flows into Cyprus, the island that houses the purple guild, which raises the question of where the guild's own servants come from.
- With Tyre at war with Assyria after the coup, Tyre's merchant-royals swallow their disgust and hire Greek ship-lords as rowers and marines.
