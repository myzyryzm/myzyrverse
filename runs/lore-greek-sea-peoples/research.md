## Background (context)

### Antiquarian: Bronze Age grounding

#### How the palaces worked, and how they broke (c. 1250–1180 BCE, historical dates)
- A Mycenaean palace was a redistribution machine. At Pylos the tablets list about 750 dependent women workers with their children, around 1,500 people, most of them fed on palace rations of wheat and figs. Bronzesmiths were given metal by the palace, and the amount handed out depended on how much bronze was in stock, not on how many smiths there were.
- Signs of strain in the last generation: the palaces built defences against a siege. Mycenae, Tiryns and Athens strengthened their Cyclopean walls and dug underground water systems, and a wall was begun across the Isthmus of Corinth that was apparently never finished. Iakovides points to a copper shortage at Pylos. One Pylos tablet (Jn 829) orders local officials to hand over bronze from the sanctuaries to be made into arrowheads and spearpoints. The exact reading is debated.
- In the final months at Pylos, coastguard units (o-ka, 'watchers') were posted at ten points along the shore, and the rower tablets call up crews of oarsmen. One tablet lists about 600 rowers, another 30 for a single ship. Scholars disagree about whether this was emergency defence or routine.
- The destructions came over a span of years, not in one night. Gla and some buildings at Thebes and Mycenae burned earlier. The Mycenae citadel, Tiryns, Midea and Pylos burned around the end of LH IIIB2 or the very start of LH IIIC, roughly 1200–1180. Pylos and Mycenae were never rebuilt as palaces.
- Proposed causes, none agreed on: drought, broken trade, peasant revolt, northern invaders, Sea Peoples raids, an 'earthquake storm', and a change in warfare from chariots to infantry. The page that summarises them notes there is no sound evidence of Sea Peoples attacks inside the Aegean itself.
- Writing ended with the palaces. Linear B disappears around 1200.

#### Drought and famine
- Pollen cores from the Larnaca Salt Lake on Cyprus and from coastal Syria point to a drought of about 300 years beginning around 1200 BCE. Farming around the lake collapsed and did not recover until about 850 BCE. The authors call the drought an amplifier of the crisis, not its only cause.
- Hatti was short of grain. Merneptah says he 'caused grain to be taken in ships, to keep alive this land of Hatti', and a Hittite king told Ugarit that a grain ship was 'a matter of life or death'. Hittite demands for grain tribute probably made Ugarit's own shortage worse not long before the city fell.

#### Post-palatial Greece (LH IIIC, c. 1190–1050 BCE)
- This was not an instant dark age. For about 50–75 years after the palaces fell, people went on building, making pottery and making figurines. Decline then came through repeated destructions over a century or more.
- **Tiryns:** in early LH IIIC a new elite laid out a planned lower town, but the project stalled and was abandoned after about two generations. A large megaron (hall) was built outside the walls in advanced LH IIIC.
- **The second wave:** Mycenae burned again around 1150/1125 BCE, and the Tiryns megaron was destroyed at about the same time. The Phylakopi shrines and the Ayia Irini temple were destroyed around 1120, possibly by an earthquake. Lefkandi has many LH IIIC destruction levels.
- **People moved.** Messenia, Laconia and parts of the Argolid emptied out. Tomb counts rose in Achaea and on Kephallenia, and the cemetery at Perati was crowded. Two waves of Mycenaean 'refugees' reached Cyprus. Short-lived fortified sites on headlands there, Maa-Palaeokastro and Pyla-Kokkinokremos, are argued (and disputed) to be refugee or raider footholds.
- **Refuges on Crete:** villages sat high on defensible peaks, among them Karphi above the Lasithi plain, Kastri, Kavousi-Kastro and Katalimata.
- **A warrior elite:** at least 24 LH IIIC warrior burials are known, 16 of them in Achaea, each with a Naue II slashing sword. By the end of LH IIIC the Naue II was the standard sword.
- **Dress and gear on pottery:** the Warrior Vase shows foot soldiers in 'hedgehog' helmets carrying small shields. Body shields and boar's-tusk helmets no longer appear after the palaces fell.
- **Art:** pictorial pottery peaked around 1160/1140–1120/1100 and was full of warriors on the march, hunts and warships. Griffins, lions and sphinxes, which had been royal emblems, now turn up in homely or playful scenes at Lefkandi. One Argive jar may parody palace writing.
- **Titles:** in Linear B the wanax was the king and the lawagetas probably the leader of the war-host. The qa-si-re-u (later basileus) was a minor local official or headman, sometimes over groups of bronzesmiths, and never a king. The same word later became the title of the kings of Cyprus.

#### Ships and raiding
- The Helladic oared galley was built for speed and has been called the most important new weapon of the Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean. It was best suited to 'raiding, piracy, and sea-based warfare'. The fifty-oar penteconter had 25 rowers a side.
- Kynos sherds (LH IIIC Middle, near modern Livanates) show ships in battle. One galley carries three warriors and a helmsman, all in feathered helmets, with nine oars and a mast and sail.
- Tactics: fast sweeps to grab whatever could easily be taken, run the ships up the beach and avoid professional troops. Nine ships might carry 360–450 fighting men. The Odyssey's raid: 'carried off the women and little children, and slew the men'.
- People were plunder even before the collapse. At Pylos one group of women is labelled ra-wi-ja-ja, 'war-captives', from a word for war-booty. Others come from Miletus, Knidos, Halikarnassos, Chios and Lemnos, though whether they were captives or refugees is unclear. A Hittite text says Alashiya was raided 'often', with captives taken.
- At Ugarit, letters speak of 'the seven ships of the enemy' and 'twenty enemy ships'. King Ammurapi complains that his own troops were away in Hatti and his ships in Lukka lands, which left his coast exposed.

#### The Sea Peoples in Egyptian records (canon diverges)
- Merneptah, Year 5 (historically about 1208): the Libyan king Meryey invaded with Sherden, Shekelesh, Ekwesh, Lukka and Teresh. Egypt claims 6,000 killed and 9,000 captured. The Egyptians cut off penises of the uncircumcised dead and hands of the circumcised, which suggests the Ekwesh were circumcised and is why their identification with Achaeans/Ahhiyawa is disputed.
- Ramesses III, Year 8 (historically about 1177): a coalition of Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen and Weshesh.
- The Sherden in Egyptian service: they were Ramesses II's bodyguard at Kadesh, and his Tanis stela says they came 'in warships from the midst of the Sea'. One text lists 520 Sherden among 5,000 troops. The later Wilbour Papyrus records 109 Sherden landholders, mostly on plots of five arourae, the size given to priests and other people of standing, and the land may have passed to their heirs.
- Ancient DNA from Philistine Ashkelon shows a southern European component arriving in the early 12th century. Greece, Crete, Sardinia and Iberia are all possible sources, and the signal had been diluted away within two centuries.
- **Divergence:** historically the Sea Peoples were a mixed group. The Lukka were Anatolian, and the Sherden and Shekelesh may have been Sardinian or Sicilian. Canon makes them all Mycenaean Greeks, so the Sherden in this world are Greek-descended.

### Psychologist: character friction

#### Note on sources and divergences
The local source folder is empty, so nothing here is cited. The history below is general knowledge offered as source material, not canon.

- **Divergence: who the Sea Peoples were.** Historically they were a mixed coalition named in Egyptian records: Sherden, Peleset, Denyen, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Weshesh, Lukka, Ekwesh. Only some of them were plausibly Aegean Greeks. Canon makes them all Mycenaean Greeks, and canon wins.
- **Divergence: the Sherden.** Historically the Sherden first show up as sea-raiders whom Ramesses II defeated. Captured men then served in his guard, at Kadesh among other places. Canon keeps the 'Ramesses II recruited Sea Peoples' core but turns them into an expensive, hereditary elite corps. The origin is also changed: Greek here, possibly Sardinian in reality.
- **Kept from history.** Ramesses III's temple reliefs show the Sea Peoples arriving by land with ox-carts full of women and children, as well as by ship. His later records boast that he settled the defeated in strongholds and fed them from his granaries. Victory and absorption were the same act.

#### Historical analogue: how a Mycenaean palace dies
- **The wanax system.** A palace like Pylos was a redistribution machine run by scribes. It collected wool, grain, oil and bronze, then handed out rations and work. Its clay tablets list rowers, smiths, women weavers paid in grain and figs, and sheep counted by the thousand. Below the wanax (king) stood the lawagetas (war-leader) and the local basileis (village headmen and work-gang overseers). The basileis were middle managers, not royalty.
- **The last tablets.** Some of the final Pylos records look like emergency measures: rowers called up, watchers posted along the coast, bronze collected from temples. They were preserved because the palace burned around them.
- **What survived the fire.** The wanax, the scribes and writing itself disappeared. The basileis did not. Within a generation the word for 'headman' had become the word for 'king'. The men who ruled the ruins were the foremen who had outlived their masters.
- **Why men took to the sea.** Bad harvests over several years, collapsing trade, palace granaries emptied or burned, and inland neighbours raiding just as hard. The ships, rowers and bronze weapons were still there. A ship could reach grain faster than a field could grow it.
- **Raiding as a family move.** The Egyptian reliefs show migration, not just warbands. A raid could be a whole household looking for land, and the line between 'pirate' and 'refugee' depended on who was telling it.

#### The Mycenaean raider-lords (the Sea Peoples as people)
**Core want:** to be wanax again: a palace, a granary, the griffin painted on the wall. **Core need:** to stop being hungry, and to put his people somewhere they can stop running.

**The lie:** 'We are taking back what the gods owe us.' Most of these men were never kings. They were headmen, rowing-masters and herd-overseers who outlived the palace. Every one of them is a usurper of a throne that no longer exists, and he knows it.

**Competing obligations:**
- Feeding the crew versus keeping oaths of guest-friendship to the coastal towns he once traded with.
- His own household versus the band. A warlord who stops raiding is replaced.
- Honouring the old gods, who failed, versus whatever answers now: purple-dregs visions, Drought omens, foreign gods.

**Pressure points:** One empty season breaks him. His men stay only as long as the hulls come home full. He is the most frightened man on his own ship. What would turn him is land to farm and a granary that is truly open to him. That is exactly what Ramesses II once offered the first Sherden.

**How he justifies his worst act** (burning a granary town, selling children to the next shore): 'The gods chose who would live through the dry years. They chose us.' Survival becomes proof of divine favour, which licenses anything.

**Friction:** He resents Egypt for having grain, and the Sherden as traitors who 'sold their oars to Pharaoh'. He fears the Cyclopes coming down from the caves and the Drought's beasts more than any army. He respects only what proves kingship, which is why canon's living griffin would undo him.

#### The Sherden (descendants of the first recruits)
Ramesses II recruited the first Sherden roughly four generations before 1160. Their descendants are comfortable and Egyptianised, holding land, but they are still visibly foreign: horned helmets, a foreign weapon, a foreign founding story.

**Core want:** to be Egyptian elite beyond question, the King's own hand. **Core need:** to make peace with the fact that the raiders their recruits are trained to kill are their cousins.

**The lie:** 'We came as guests; they come as thieves.' The founding legend in their families surely softens whatever their great-grandfathers actually did on the Delta coast.

**Competing obligations:**
- Loyalty to Pharaoh versus the duty to train the paying sons of the nobles who resent him.
- Hereditary Sherden sons versus bought-in royal and noble recruits.
- A lingering kinship with the Greek tongue and gods versus a career that depends on despising both.

**Pressure points:** A captured raider who speaks their grandmother's words. A noble patron offering what Pharaoh's wars never paid. If the super-soldier program is made real (still open), it means dosing their own blood-kin, a secret that could split the corps.

**Friction:** Their contempt for the Medjay is the same anxiety turned outward. A corps afraid of being seen as foreign mercenaries makes a point of looking down on the native servants of the state.

#### Jon
**Core want:** to earn a place among the Sherden on merit, not as a pharaoh's spare son. **Core need:** to see the people he is being trained to kill as people.

**The lie:** 'Raiders are wildlings; we are the wall.' Jon learns his picture of the Greeks from Sherden instructors who have their own reasons to make them monstrous.

**Competing obligations:** Loyalty to the corps' practice versus his own judgment. Canon already shows him refusing to kill or cage the griffin. Later come his royal blood, his father's war plans, and whoever is starving in front of him.

**Pressure points:** The Greek world he crosses is the inside of the Sea Peoples story: palaces that were once great, now broken, and families on the move. His arc toward leading refugees into Egypt mirrors the founding act of the corps that trained him.

**Friction:** The Sherden-born recruits resent a prince's bought place. Any Greek he meets will see the horned helmet and read 'Pharaoh's dog'.

#### Ramesses III
**Core want:** to be remembered as Ramesses II's equal. His reign's greatest boast is that he threw back the Sea Peoples and the Libyans. **Core need:** to accept that he is the caretaker of a shrinking world, not a conqueror.

**The lie:** 'I broke them.' In fact he fought a hungry tide to a halt and then had to absorb and feed what was left, as his historical counterpart did. His guard is made of the enemy's descendants.

**Pressure points:** Any reminder that his 'great victory' was defensive, expensive and paid for by the nobles. The Assyrian war is partly a way to get a conquest that the Sea Peoples war never gave him.

**Friction:** He trusts the Sherden with his body because they owe everything to the crown, and he forgets that men who owe everything can be bought by whoever promises more.

#### Secondary figures the brief touches
- **The Medjay farm boy.** He sees the Sea Peoples, and the Sherden, as foreigners paid better than native sons for the same blood. His want is wealth and standing. His lie is that Egypt belongs to farmers like his father. That is complicated once refugees arrive, and by any Greek-born Sherden who outranks him.
- **Isis and Tyre.** Sea Peoples raids are one reason Tyre's merchant fleet is so valuable and so threatened. Isis's entitlement includes a merchant-royal disgust for raiders: they are the opposite of trade. Tyre may still hire them as rowers and marines when it suits.
- **Tiye's possible Sherden lover, and Tywin if he is a Sherden commander (both open).** A rising Sherden soldier carries the corps' buried status anxiety. That rhymes with Tiye's own: two outsiders both made to feel their low origins at court.

#### Friction map
- **Raider-lords ↔ Sherden:** Kin who each think the other betrayed what they were. The raiders call the Sherden kept dogs; the Sherden call the raiders animals. Each is the other's possible future.
- **Sherden ↔ Medjay:** Foreign-born elite against native service, each insecure about the very thing the other has: royal favour or native belonging.
- **Jon ↔ Sherden instructors:** He inherits their contempt for the Greeks and then outgrows it. His refusals read to them as softness, or as a prince's arrogance.
- **Jon ↔ raider-lords:** He looks like a Sherden, which to them means traitor-kin. He is also a pharaoh's son, which makes him a hostage worth a fortune.
- **Ramesses III ↔ Sherden:** Mutual dependence that he mistakes for loyalty.
- **Ramesses III ↔ the nobles:** The Sea Peoples war is the bill they are still angry about.
- **Raider-lords ↔ each other:** A rival basileus is the man who might feed your crew better, so every alliance is an 'enemy of my enemy' truce with an expiry date.
- **Isis/Tyre ↔ raiders:** Disgust and dependence: the raiders threaten the trade, and they are also the cheapest rowers in the sea.

### Historian: historical analogues

#### The real record: what happened to the Mycenaean Greeks (and where canon diverges)
- **The palaces dug in before they fell.** Late in their last building phase, Mycenae, Tiryns and Athens all added stronger walls and secret underground water supplies, the kind you build to survive a long siege. A wall across the Isthmus of Corinth, meant to block a land invasion of the Peloponnese, seems never to have been finished.
- **The fires.** Mycenae, Tiryns, Midea, Thebes and Pylos were destroyed, mostly by fire, around the turn from LH IIIB to IIIC. Pylos and Mycenae were never rebuilt as palaces. Tiryns may have been hit by an earthquake, and its lower town was flooded. These dates are relative and loose.
- **Pylos saw it coming, maybe.** One set of tablets lists men posted at ten points to watch the coast. Another records about 600 rowers called away from their home districts. Some scholars read these as preparations for an attack from the sea, though others disagree. The watch lists line up with landholding records, so military duty seems to have been owed in exchange for land.
- **The palace fed a dependent workforce.** Pylos rationed about 750 women and their children, most of them textile workers. Some came from Miletus, Knidos, Halikarnassos, Chios, Lemnos and Aswia (probably later Lydia), and one group is labelled 'war-captives'. Chadwick argued that most were bought in eastern Aegean slave markets. The palace economy already depended on people taken from across the sea.
- **What came after.** Two waves of Mycenaean 'refugees' reached Cyprus (Enkomi, Kition, Maa, Sinda). Achaea and the Ionian islands, especially Kephallenia, filled with new tombs, and people crowded into places like Perati in Attica. Pots of the period show warriors in chariot scenes, and Deger-Jalkotzy's model of roaming warrior bands is the main explanation for this world.
- **The other side of the sea.** Ammurapi, the last king of Ugarit, wrote to the king of Alashiya (Cyprus) that enemy ships were burning his towns while his own troops, chariots and ships were away elsewhere.
- **Egypt's list of names.** Ramesses III's Year 8 text at Medinet Habu names a coalition of Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen and Weshesh. The Sherden are not named among the invaders in that account.
- **Sherden precedent (matches canon).** Ramesses II's Tanis stele from Pi-Ramesses tells of 'rebellious' Sherden who came in warships, probably on a coastal raid. They were captured and taken to Egypt, and 'Sherden of His Majesty's capturing' later appear as royal troops at Kadesh.
- **Divergence labels:** Historically the Sea Peoples were a mix of peoples (Sherden possibly linked to Sardinia, for example), and the Mycenaean collapse has no single cause. Canon makes the Sea Peoples Mycenaean Greeks and names the Drought as the driving force. The real drought theory exists but rests on thin Aegean data.

#### Analogue: the Cilician pirates (2nd–1st c. BCE)
- **What happened.** Piracy started small in Cilicia, grew bold serving Mithridates, and spread while Rome's civil wars left the sea unguarded. The pirates moved from taking ships to raiding islands and coastal cities.
- **Mechanics.** They ran fortified harbours and signal stations with fast, skilled crews. Plutarch puts them at more than 1,000 ships and says they took 400 cities. They looted sanctuaries and held Roman officials for ransom. Men from good families joined because piracy brought them standing. They had become the only real naval power in the eastern Mediterranean.
- **How it ended.** In 67 BCE Pompey split the sea into 13 commands and drove the fleets back to Cilicia. Pirates who surrendered early were spared and used to hunt down the rest, and the job took under three months.
- **The settlement.** Pompey spared more than 20,000 men on the reasoning that they were made wild by circumstance, not born wild. He moved them inland: some to Soli, rebuilt as Pompeiopolis, and most to the emptied town of Dyme in Achaea.
- **What transfers.** Piracy grows where the great naval power has stopped policing the sea. Raiders can be bought off with land, and a commander who forgives early can turn raiders against each other.

#### Analogue: the wokou and the Ming sea ban (14th–16th c.)
- **What happened.** From 1371 the Ming banned private sea trade to stamp out piracy. The ban could not be enforced, so smuggling and piracy became endemic.
- **Mechanics.** Legitimate coastal traders were made criminals by law. Wang Zhi began as a salt merchant, became a smuggler, and in 1544 joined a syndicate on Shuangyu. When restrictions tightened in 1551, he sent his fleets to loot the coast. The 'Japanese pirates' of the great 1550s raids were mostly Chinese.
- **How it ended.** Campaigns in 1548–1557 killed many leaders, but piracy only became negligible after the ban was lifted in 1567. Wang Zhi was executed in 1560 after coming ashore to bargain for the ban to be relaxed.
- **What transfers.** When trade shrinks or is shut off, traders and sailors turn raider, and the label 'foreign pirates' hides how many of them are locals. Raiding falls once legal trade returns.

#### Analogue: the Goths at the Danube (376–378 CE)
- **What happened.** Gothic groups pushed from the north were allowed to cross the Danube in 376. They came over in boats, on rafts and in hollowed tree-trunks, and many drowned.
- **Mechanics.** Starving after the crossing, they were exploited by the Roman commanders Lupicinus and Maximus, who traded one dog for one slave, chieftains' sons included. Lupicinus then invited the Gothic leaders Alavivus and Fritigern to a banquet at Marcianopolis while his soldiers kept their people outside the walls. Fighting broke out, and the Goths destroyed a Roman troop.
- **How it ended.** The refugees became an army, and Ammianus goes on in the same book to the disaster at Adrianople.
- **What transfers.** Refugees whom the host state exploits at the frontier become a fighting force. The border officials who profit from starving migrants are more dangerous to the state than the migrants are.

#### Analogue: the Vikings, from 'bare branches' to Normandy
- **What drove them.** Raffield, Price and Collard argue that polygyny, concubinage and a widening gap between rich and poor in late Iron Age Scandinavia left too few marriageable women for the men. Young men with no prospects raided for wealth and status, and great men led expeditions for loot and reputation, so raiding served both. They stress that this was a tendency, not something every man did.
- **How it ended in Normandy.** After defeat at Chartres in 911, Rollo got the land between the Epte and the sea. In return he gave loyalty, was baptised, and swore to defend the Seine against other Vikings. The terms come mainly from Dudo, writing a century later, and only one charter, from 918, mentions the grant.
- **What transfers.** Raiding can be driven by men with no land and no marriage prospects as much as by hunger. The usual endgame is a raider turned licensed gatekeeper against other raiders.

#### Dynamics that transfer to the Myzyrverse
- **The war-band takes over the storehouse's job.** Rowers, coast-watchers and ration-fed workers all depended on the palace. When it burned, the war-band became the new source of food and standing.
- **Raiding as an audition.** Captured Sherden became pharaoh's guard, Pompey's pirates became farmers and Rollo became a duke. Every warlord can see that raiding hard enough gets you bought.
- **Closing trade makes pirates.** The sea ban is the model for any power that shuts its ports when trade is already shrinking.
- **Mistreated refugees become armies.** Frontier officials who sell food for children start the next war.
- **The slave trade reverses.** Mycenaean palaces bought Anatolian women. Their heirs now take people and sell them instead.
- **Young men without land or wives.** In a drought, hoarded land and hoarded women add to the pool of desperate men.

### Mythographer: fictional analogues

#### Epic (fiction): the Odyssey's pirate-hero and the Cretan lie
- In Odyssey 14, the disguised Odysseus tells the swineherd Eumaeus a made-up life story. In it he is a Cretan sea-captain who raids Egypt. His crews plunder the farms, carry off the women and children and kill the men. The Egyptians strike back and the fleet is broken. The pharaoh spares the narrator, who then lives in Egypt for seven years and grows rich. Some scholars read the tale as a folk memory of Sea Peoples raids on the Delta.
- Book 9 covers the same ground. Odysseus's first act after Troy is to sack Ismarus for loot. He then raids a Cyclops's cave for cheese and sheep.
- What it does: raiding is ordinary heroic work, not villainy. The hero is a pirate, and the poem never apologises for it.
- Why it works: listeners get a teller who is charming, lies, and can be both victim and predator. The 'lie' itself carries truth about how such men lived.
- Pitfalls: it romanticises the raider. If the story is told only from the raider's side, the people on the burned farms disappear.
- Transform: the Cretan lie is already a Greek raider's account of failing against Egypt. Turn it around so that Egyptian and Greek characters tell the same raid in opposite ways.

#### Myth (fiction): the Nostoi, or the age of bad homecomings
- The lost epic Nostoi told what happened to the Greek heroes after Troy. Many drowned in Poseidon's storm. Diomedes came home to an unfaithful wife and was driven out to Italy. Idomeneus was expelled from Crete by a usurper. Teucer was rejected at Salamis and founded a new Salamis on Cyprus.
- Thucydides, writing centuries later, turns this into a theory of history. After Troy, Greece was unsettled by migration and faction. Exiles founded new cities. The Dorians and Heraclids took the Peloponnese a few generations later.
- In Hesiod's ages of man, the race of heroes who fought at Thebes and Troy is followed by the Iron race of toil and injustice.
- What it does: the collapse becomes a story about men. The great venture abroad drains home, and the men who come back find no home to return to.
- Why it works: a victory that ruins the winner is tragic irony. Exile also produces wandering stories, which suits a road narrative.
- Pitfalls: it turns a systemic catastrophe (drought, trade, famine) into the private sins of kings. 'One war caused everything' is too neat.
- Transform: in this world the failed venture abroad does not have to be Troy. It can be the invasions of Egypt and Hatti.

#### Fiction (ASOIAF): the Ironborn and the Old Way
- The Ironborn's words are 'We Do Not Sow'. Under the Old Way, goods worth having are taken by 'the iron price', meaning raiding, not bought for 'the gold price'. Thralls can only be taken in a raid, never purchased. Captive women become 'salt wives'. Farm labour falls to thralls and the low-born.
- Since the Old Way was crushed, the Ironborn have kept trying to restore it.
- What it does: raiding becomes an ideology and an identity, not just a way to eat. Defeat feeds nostalgia for a purer age.
- Why it works: readers grasp a culture through its slogans and taboos at once. A people defined by contempt for farmers produces both menace and a pathos of decline.
- Pitfalls: the Ironborn are often read as GRRM's thinnest culture: one note, cruel, and Viking in costume. Their ideology has no believable cause beyond poor soil. Copying it would make Greek raiders cartoon Vikings, which is medieval-adjacent rather than Bronze Age.

#### Fiction (ASOIAF): the Free Folk, refugees who look like invaders
- Seen from the Wall, the wildlings are raiders. Mance Rayder unites the quarrelling clans because the Others are driving them south. Mance's host is a whole people on the move, not an army of conquest.
- After they are defeated, Jon Snow negotiates their passage through the Wall in exchange for help defending it. At Hardhome the rescue is overtaken by the dead.
- What it does: the reveal recasts the enemy as victims of a greater enemy. It also hands the hero a moral choice: let the barbarians in.
- Why it works: readers like having a judgement overturned, and the choice divides the hero's own side.
- Pitfalls: the noble-savage flip, where the raiders become simply good. 'They were refugees all along' is now a cliché, since wildlings already cover it.
- Historical hinge (history, not fiction): Ramesses III's Medinet Habu reliefs show the land host of his Year 8 war with women and children in ox-carts. Many scholars read it as a migration, though some see a coordinated invasion. In this canon Ramesses III's reign begins around 1185, so his Year 8 falls around 1177, roughly 17 years before the story.

#### Fiction (film): Mad Max: Fury Road, the water-lord
- Immortan Joe rules the Citadel by hoarding the water. He lets it out in timed bursts and tells the crowd below not to become 'addicted to water'. His war boys are a cult of young men who die for him.
- What it does: scarcity becomes politics. Whoever holds the water makes the rules, and everyone outside the walls is either a client or a scavenger.
- Why it works: the logic is immediate and physical. Climate collapse is shown as a social order, not a weather report.
- Pitfalls: post-apocalyptic chrome, grotesque spectacle, and a villain who is too obviously monstrous.
- Historical hinge (history, not fiction): in the late 13th century BCE, Mycenae extended its walls and dug an underground cistern fed from the Perseia spring, to secure water in a siege. Tiryns doubled its enclosed area and storage and also secured its water. The Linear B palace archives, mostly inventories and rations, disappear with the palaces, and writing is forgotten in Greece for centuries.

## Proposals

### The raiders are the palaces' own war-fleet, cut loose (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Sea Peoples, Mycenaean warlords, The Drought, Sherden, Jon's exodus

**World**: The Sea Peoples are not outsiders who wrecked Greece. They are what was left of the palaces' own sea-power once the Drought broke the ration system that paid for it. In the last years each wanax called up rowers and posted coastguards. When the grain and bronze stopped coming, the crews kept the ships and went on doing what palaces had always done on the side, raiding Anatolian and Cypriot coasts for goods and captive women, only now for themselves. Leadership passed to the small men: local headmen (qa-si-re-u) and ship-captains whose rank is counted in hulls and oars, each holding a bit of ruined palace and calling himself king. A galley of fifty oars is a warband of fifty.

**Integration**: This fits 'warlords fighting each other' and 'pirate wildlings', and gives the canon Drought a concrete way of causing the collapse. Divergence: historically the Sea Peoples were mixed, and no Aegean destruction can be pinned on them. Making them Mycenaean palace remnants is a deliberate choice.
