## Background (context)

### Antiquarian: Bronze Age grounding

#### The historical queen behind Isis
- The real Great Royal Wife of Ramesses III was Iset Ta-Hemdjert, called simply Isis in her tomb. Her titles were Great Royal Wife and God's Wife, and later King's Mother under her son Ramesses VI.
- Her father is unknown. Her mother's name is recorded as Hemdjert, Habadjilat or Hebnerdjent. Dodson and Hilton read it as Syrian rather than Egyptian, so she is often thought to be of Asiatic (Levantine) descent. This is an inference from the name alone.
- Who was really Ramesses III's principal wife is disputed. Some scholars give that place to Queen Tyti, whose son succeeded him. Canon has already settled this: Isis is the Great Royal Wife and a princess of Tyre.
- Her tomb is QV51 in the Valley of the Queens. It was begun under Ramesses III, finished under Ramesses VI, and robbed in antiquity by Deir el-Medina workmen, who were later questioned under Ramesses IX.

#### How foreign brides came to Egypt
- Egypt took in foreign princesses but would not send its own daughters out. A Babylonian king quotes Pharaoh's refusal: from of old, no daughter of the king of Egypt has been given to anyone.
- The closest model for Isis is Maathorneferure, the Hittite bride of Ramesses II in his Year 34. Her parents made the rank of King's Great Wife a condition of the marriage, which Ramesses' other foreign wives never got.
- Her marriage took long bargaining. When Ramesses complained about the delay, Queen Puduhepa answered that a proper dowry took time to gather. The letters show the two courts dealing almost as equals, while Egypt's own Marriage Stela calls the match a Hittite submission.
- Her caravan carried gold, silver, bronze, cattle, sheep and slaves, with Hittite troops guarding it. Egyptian soldiers met it and escorted it through Canaan to Pi-Ramesses. She received her father's envoys at court for a while, then seems to have been moved to the harem palace at Mer-wer (Gurob). Some scholars read that as Ramesses sidelining her.
- She was given an Egyptian name, Maathorneferure, roughly 'one who sees Horus, the splendour of Ra'.
- Foreign brides came with large households. The Mitannian princess Gilukhepa arrived for Amenhotep III with 317 women, and she still ranked below his common-born chief wife, Tiye.

#### The royal women's household (khener)
- The khener, usually translated 'harem', was an administrative institution of royal women attached to palaces. It was paid for by taxation and drew regular rations.
- It housed Egyptian wives and their children along with the foreign wives the king married for diplomacy. Some wives and children probably lived in the kap, a palace nursery and school.
- Mer-wer (Gurob) in the Faiyum was a separate harem-palace, founded under Thutmose III and still in use in the late Ramesside period.
- The model is a royal estate with its own officials, not an Ottoman seraglio.

#### Tyre in the Late Bronze Age
- Tyre was an island city. An Egyptian satirical letter of Ramesses II's time says of it: 'another city that lies in the sea, the port of Tyre is its name. Water is carried to it by ship'.
- It depended on its mainland town, Usu, for water, wood and burial ground. When Sidon's ruler seized Usu, Tyre's prince wrote that 'we have neither water nor wood' and had no place to bury the dead.
- About 1350 BCE Tyre was an Egyptian vassal. Its prince, Abimilku, called Pharaoh 'my lord, my god, my sun' and fell at his feet 'seven times and seven times'. He held ships ready for Egyptian troops, reported news from the north, and spent much of his correspondence on his feud with Sidon.
- A Ugaritic letter from a king of Tyre to the king of Ugarit reports that a Ugaritian ship bound for Egypt was caught in a storm off Tyre. Tyre took charge of its crew and cargo and sent word, which shows Tyre's kings working as harbour-masters of the coast.
- Excavation at Tyre is described as giving a continuous sequence from the Early Bronze Age onward. However, its Bronze and Iron Age levels are poorly known because of later building and the sea-level rise.
- Divergence: historically it is Sidon that looks strongest in the early Iron Age. In the Wenamun story, fifty ships at Sidon trade for a merchant house there. Tyre's great age comes later. Canon's dominant Tyre in 1160 is a deliberate change.

#### Purple, gods and the collapse next door
- Sarepta, on the coast between Tyre and Sidon, has three large heaps of crushed Murex trunculus. They date to Late Bronze II and to the Late Bronze–Iron I transition, so dye-making nearby continued through the collapse.
- Ugarit paid tribute to the Hittites in purple wool.
- Ugarit was destroyed around 1192–1190 BCE. Its last king, Ammurapi, wrote that his troops and chariots were in Hittite country and his ships in Lukka while enemy ships burned his towns. The relief troops arrived after the city had fallen.
- Melqart, 'Lord of Tyre', is the city's god in later times, but the clear inscriptions date from the 9th century BCE. For a court around 1200–1160, 'the Lord (Baal) of Tyre' and Astarte are the safer forms. Canon already treats the Melqart's-dog myth as a later civic claim.

### Psychologist: character friction

- **Historical analogue:** pharaohs took foreign princesses as wives but rarely made them Great Royal Wife. The best-known exception is Ramesses II raising his Hittite bride. A foreign-born Great Royal Wife is therefore unusual. Her position depends on politics, and she would know that.
- **Timeline from canon ages:** Isis is ~45 and Ramesses III has reigned ~25 years. She probably married around the start of the reign (~1185–1180), at about 20. When the common-born Tiye arrived (~1175, aged 15), Isis was about 30 and had already borne the heir.

#### Isis: core analysis
**What she wants:** the deference she was born to. Above all, she wants that deference to prove her marriage was worth what it cost her. She also wants Tyre safe, and those two wants have always pointed the same way.

**What she needs:** to find out whether she is worth anything apart from her blood. She is a merchant-house royal, raised among people who keep ledgers, and she is probably shrewder than her pride lets her show.

**The lie she tells herself:** "Royalty is in the blood, and blood can't be faked." Her husband's claim is thin: he is the son of a usurping general and "a second son of a second son of a third daughter." She came from an old, rich house. So the lie protects her from the thought that she married down, and that Egypt needed her more than she needed Egypt.

**Obligations she can't honor all at once:**
- daughter of Tyre or queen of Egypt;
- her children as Egypt's heirs or as Tyre's stake in Egypt;
- defending Aegon or admitting he is unfit.

**Pressure points:**
- Aegon's failure. If her blood can produce an unfit heir, then blood isn't enough.
- Being forced to need Tiye or Pentawer.
- Tyre's survival depending on someone she despises.
- Her house treating her as spent capital once Egypt is lost.

**How she justifies her worst act:** "I am owed. I paid for this crown with my life, my homeland and my sons." Any cruelty to Tiye is "only the order of things."

#### Ramesses III, as Isis sees him
- He is a general's son chasing Ramesses II's ghost. She shares his hunger for legacy, but for her it is a family debt, not a dream.
- The Assyrian war is the one place their wants fully line up. The marriage may work best as a war council.
- They split over Aegon. Ramesses despises the boy's lack of martial interest. Isis can't agree without admitting her blood failed.
- His taking Tiye, a commoner, after Isis had already given him heirs read to her as a declaration that her rank was not needed.

#### Tiye, Aegon, Helen and Jon in relation to Isis
- **Tiye:** came to court at 15, naive, while Isis was 30 and secure. Isis's contempt was probably routine to her and easy to forget. To Tiye it was formative and unforgettable. The coup also reverses Isis's foreign policy, so it is aimed at her as much as at the king.
- **Aegon:** her heir and her mirror. His degeneracy is the evidence against her creed, so she has every reason to blame others for it: his father, his companions, the black-market purple sellers.
- **Helen:** married to Aegon in a loveless sibling match. In Egyptian practice that keeps the throne wholly inside the Great Royal Wife's line. Whatever Helen wants, the match serves Isis.
- **Jon:** the son who chose the hard road among Sherden-born men. He gives her nothing to defend and nothing to blame, which may make him the child she understands least.

#### Tyre's royal house
- Tyre's lever over everyone is the murex beds. It resents the purple guild for making what Tyre cannot. Isis would have grown up hearing about that resentment.
- After the coup, she learns of her husband's death through the guild's relay, the same network that carried the conspirators' messages without a word of warning. For a Tyrian, that betrayal would hurt in two ways at once.
- Her brother, or whichever of her sons leads Tyre against Assyria, will be weighing her worth. Once Egypt is lost to her, is she a returning princess or a liability?

#### Friction map
- **Isis and Tiye:** contempt on one side, a grievance tended for fifteen years on the other. Isis misreads Tiye as still the naive girl. Neither will admit that each married a crown for her family's sake.
- **Isis and Ramesses III:** allies on the war, rivals over the heir. She resents needing his throne. He resents needing her city.
- **Isis and Aegon:** she defends him in public and is ashamed of him in private. He may sense that her protection is about her, not him.
- **Isis and Helen:** mother and instrument. Helen's loveless marriage is Isis's design.
- **Isis and Jon:** distance. The son least shaped by her may be the one who most resembles her in competence.
- **Isis and the Tyrian house:** blood loyalty. But once the Egyptian investment fails, she is a returned debt.
- **Isis and the purple guild:** inherited Tyrian resentment, sharpened by the guild's neutrality during the coup.
- **Isis and the nobles:** they pay for the war she pushes and cannot say so. She takes their silence for consent.
- **Isis and Pentawer:** the common-born woman's son on her children's throne, married to the enemy of her homeland. He is the living refutation of everything she believes.

### Historian: historical analogues

#### Jezebel: a Tyrian princess married abroad (9th c. BCE)
What happened: Jezebel was a daughter of Ithobaal I, king of Tyre. The Bible calls him king of the 'Sidonians', a general word for Phoenicians. She married Ahab of Israel after a period of friendly relations between Israel and Phoenicia, and one scholar calls the match a 'brilliant stroke of international diplomacy'. Classical genealogies, which are not reliable, make her a great-aunt of Dido of Carthage.

Mechanics: She brought her homeland's cult with her, and Ahab built a Baal altar in Samaria for her. Phoenician royal women were sometimes priestesses of Astarte, which would have given her an active role at court. She used royal machinery against a subject, arranging Naboth's execution on false charges so the king could take his vineyard.

Outcome: Her sons Ahaziah and Jehoram both reigned. Jehu's coup killed her son and the allied king of Judah, then came for her at Jezreel. She put on cosmetics and a formal wig and taunted him from a window, which is read either as defiance or as a deliberate display of royal dignity. Her own eunuchs threw her down.

Dynamics that transfer:
- A Tyrian queen abroad is resented as the face of a foreign house and foreign gods.
- Her pride stays intact even in defeat.
- A coup against her husband's line goes through her household servants.
- Canon diverges here: Isis survives and is away. Jezebel was present and killed.

#### Olympias of Epirus: the older-blooded queen and the new wife (4th c. BCE)
What happened: Olympias was a Molossian princess whose house claimed descent from Achilles's son. Her 357 BCE marriage to Philip II sealed an alliance between Macedon and Epirus.

Mechanics of the rift: In 337 BCE Philip married a Macedonian noblewoman, Cleopatra Eurydice. At the wedding feast her guardian Attalus questioned Alexander's legitimacy, and Philip did not defend his son. Olympias and Alexander withdrew to her brother's court in Epirus.

Outcome: Philip was murdered in 336 BCE. Olympias was suspected of backing the killing. Later she corresponded with Alexander while he was on campaign, quarrelled with the regent Antipater, and went back to Epirus. In 317 BCE she returned with Epirote and Macedonian backing and won over the opposing army. She executed the rival king Philip III and his queen, was then besieged at Pydna, and was killed despite promises that her life would be spared.

Dynamics that transfer:
- A queen whose blood is older than her husband's treats any new wife's rise as an insult to the line itself.
- A public slight to her son's legitimacy drives her home to her birth family.
- Her absence during the killing makes her both safe and a suspect.
- Her homeland becomes the base for a return.

#### Bronze Age marriage diplomacy: Amarna and the Hittite bride
What happened: Pharaohs collected foreign princesses. Amenhotep III tried to take a wife from each fellow king. Egypt refused to send its own daughters abroad. A Babylonian king complained in a letter that he had been told 'never has a daughter of the king of Egypt been given to anyone'.

Mechanics:
- Foreign brides could vanish into the harem. Amenhotep III had to assure Babylon that its princess was alive and had not been demoted to a distant harem. Even so, the Babylonian envoys could not recognise her.
- When Ramesses II married a daughter of the Hittite king Hattusili III and Queen Puduhepa around his Year 34, the Hittite side's key demand was that she hold the rank of King's Great Wife.
- Puduhepa personally negotiated the dowry and insisted her daughter go 'in style'. One account says Egypt valued the dowry more than the bride.
- The journey to Pi-Ramesses was carved on a marriage stela at Abu Simbel.
- She shared the title with several of Ramesses II's own daughters, and her later life is obscure.

Dynamics that transfer:
- A foreign Great Royal Wife's rank is something her family won by contract, not a gift from her husband.
- Her dowry is a debt the crown carries.
- Without constant pressure from home she can still be quietly sidelined.
- Canon diverges here: historically, Ramesses III's chief queen had no known foreign origin.

#### Tyre as a humble vassal: Abi-Milku's letters (14th c. BCE)
What happened: Two centuries before the story, the ruler of Tyre, Abi-Milku, sent at least ten tablets (EA 146–155) to Akhenaten. Scholars often call him a governor rather than a king.

Mechanics: Island Tyre depended on the mainland town of Usu for water. Under pressure from Sidon, Abi-Milku wrote that he was guarding Tyre for the king 'until the powerful arm of the king comes forth… to give me water to drink and wood to warm myself'. After losing Usu he reported that he had 'neither water nor wood', and nowhere to bury the dead.

Dynamics that transfer:
- In living memory of the archives, Tyre's rulers addressed pharaoh as servants begging for water.
- A Tyrian princess who is now richer than her husband's nobles carries that reversal in her pride.
- The Egyptian scribal archive still holds proof of Tyre's former abasement.

#### Mahidevran and Hürrem: rank versus access (Ottoman, 16th c.)
This is the canon's situation reversed. Mahidevran was Suleiman's senior consort and mother of the heir, Mustafa. Hürrem was a favourite who rose to become his legal wife.

Mechanics:
- By about 1526 Suleiman's affection had shifted to Hürrem.
- The two women's rivalry was held in check while Suleiman's mother lived.
- One report has Mahidevran beating Hürrem in a fight, which angered Suleiman, so her pride cost her favour.

Outcome: In 1553 Suleiman had Mustafa executed for alleged plotting against him. Hürrem and the grand vizier Rüstem were suspected of involvement, but it was never proved. Mahidevran retired to Bursa in reduced circumstances. By one account she got a state pension only under her rival's son.

Dynamics that transfer:
- The higher-ranked consort trusts rank and her son's place in line.
- The lower-born rival works through the ruler's affection and his ministers.
- Open displays of contempt hand the rival the moral high ground.
- Afterwards the senior consort may depend on the new regime's charity.

#### Elisabeth Farnese and Marie Antoinette: the queen who steers foreign policy
Elisabeth Farnese (Spain, 18th c.):
- She was the second wife of Philip V. Her sons stood behind stepsons in the Spanish succession, so she turned Spanish policy toward winning Italian thrones for them, which led to several wars.
- The match was brokered by Alberoni, the Duke of Parma's agent in Madrid, who then became Spain's effective chief minister until 1719.
- Her son Carlos took Parma in 1732 and later Naples and Sicily.

Marie Antoinette (France, 18th c.):
- Libels painted her as profligate and sympathetic to her native Austria.
- Austria's ambassador Mercy-Argenteau kept up a secret correspondence reporting on her to her mother.
- When war came in 1792 she was accused of passing military secrets to Austria. She was convicted of high treason and executed in 1793.
- She later suspected Mercy and the emperor of sacrificing her to Austria's interests.

Dynamics that transfer:
- A foreign queen comes with a homeland agent who rises at court.
- War that serves her homeland or her sons gets blamed on her personally.
- Her birth family watches her, and may sacrifice her when it suits them.

### Mythographer: fictional analogues

#### Jezebel: the Tyrian princess at a foreign court (scripture and legend)
Jezebel is the closest literary model there is: a princess of Tyre married into a foreign kingdom. In 1 and 2 Kings she is the daughter of Ithobaal (also written Ethbaal), priest-king of Tyre and Sidon, and her marriage to Ahab of Israel is usually read as a trade alliance between the two. This is a biblical text set roughly three centuries after 1160, not a record of this story's era. It is used here as a literary analogue only.

What the device does:
- She arrives with her own gods and her own household. Ahab builds a Baal altar for her, and she sponsors Baal and Asherah worship.
- She uses royal power without restraint. When Naboth refuses to sell his vineyard, she has him killed on false charges.
- After her husband's line falls to the usurper Jehu, she dresses in her finery, does her face, and meets him at a window in defiance. He has her thrown down, and dogs eat her body.

Why it works: she stays a queen to the last moment. The foreign wife who will not bend to her new country is a clear, dramatic opponent for any court.

Pitfalls: the text was written by her enemies. Her name became shorthand for idolatry and sexual vice, and modern scholars read that portrait as hostile framing. Copying it as written produces a cardboard foreign seductress.

#### Dido/Elissa: Tyre's royal family as a nest of knives (legend and epic)
Legend, not history. The earliest source is Timaeus, writing centuries after the events, and Virgil's Aeneid is fiction built on that legend.

- **The story:** the king of Tyre leaves the crown to his children Elissa and Pygmalion jointly, but the people give it to Pygmalion alone. He then murders Elissa's husband, a rich priest of Melqart, to get his treasure. She escapes with the gold and with Tyrians who oppose her brother. In some versions she tricks Pygmalion's men by throwing sacks overboard that she says hold the treasure. She goes on to found Carthage.
- **What it does:** it presents Tyre's royal house as a merchant dynasty where kinship, money and temple office are tangled together. A princess is both a family asset and a possible rival claimant.
- **Why it works:** a woman who is exiled with the family treasure, and who negotiates rather than fights, is a strong survival arc. Dido strikes a land deal with a Libyan king when she arrives.
- **Pitfalls:** Virgil turns her into a lovesick queen who kills herself for Aeneas, which is the cliché to avoid. The older legend is sharper: she chose death rather than a forced marriage.

#### Cersei Lannister: entitlement backed by a rich house (fiction: ASOIAF / Game of Thrones)
- **Source:** Cersei is the only daughter of Tywin, the immensely wealthy Lord of Casterly Rock, married to King Robert for politics. She grew up largely without parents and was raised to be a lady while her twin was raised as heir.
- **What it does:** her pride rests on her family's gold rather than on anything she has done herself. A childhood prophecy feeds a paranoia that helps make the prophecy come true.
- **Why it works:** readers enjoy a character whose contempt for others hides deep fear. Her snobbery is never just snobbery.
- **Pitfalls:** the canon's Isis already shares the obvious traits (a rich house, entitlement, a loveless royal marriage, children who echo her). A straight copy would read as fan fiction. The characters are also already partly named after ASOIAF (Tywin, Olenna), so the Cersei echo should be bent, not repeated. Cersei is also the schemer of her story, and in this story the schemer is Tiye. Isis's role is the opposite one: the queen the coup is aimed at.

#### Katherine of Aragon in Wolf Hall: dignity as a weapon (historical fiction)
These are Hilary Mantel's novels, a fictionalized version of the real queen.

- **Source:** Katherine is a foreign princess and a deposed queen, kept under house arrest with a keeper. Her daughter Mary is likewise confined and is the alternative heir.
- **What it does:** in Bring Up the Bodies, Cromwell visits her at Kimbolton and urges compromise. She refuses to bend while Anne is queen, and she will not be addressed as anything less than queen; Cromwell calls her only "madam".
- **Why it works:** a woman with no army, whose only weapon is refusing to acknowledge her enemy, is formidable and tragic at once. Every scene with her becomes a negotiation over titles and forms of address.
- **Pitfalls:** pure saintly stubbornness gets static. Mantel keeps it alive by giving her grief, such as a first son who lived only a few weeks, and a quick intelligence.

#### Hecuba: the queen who survives the fall (Greek tragedy)
Two plays by Euripides, both fiction built on the Troy myth.

- **Trojan Women:** the former queen of Troy waits among the ruins while her women are shared out as captives and her grandson is killed. The play is almost entirely her grief.
- **Hecuba:** grief turns into action. Learning that her youngest son was murdered by a Thracian king she trusted, she lures him into her tent, kills his sons and blinds him.
- **What it does:** it shows the survivor queen's path running from mourning to revenge.
- **Why it works:** a queen who loses everything except her will is about as old a tragic figure as there is.
- **Pitfalls:** endless lament. In this story's canon no royal children die in the coup, so Isis's losses are her husband, her status and her country's policy, not her children. The pure-mourning version of Hecuba fits poorly.

## Proposals

### Royalty only two generations old (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Isis, Tiye, the nobility, Tyre

**Characterization**: Isis insists on royal precedence because Tyre's royalty is new and fragile. In the Amarna letters, Tyre's rulers were Pharaoh's vassals: they fell 'seven times and seven times', called him 'my sun', and begged for water and firewood for an island that could not feed itself. Old Egyptian families still remember that. Her generation turned a vassal port into a house that Pharaoh marries as an equal. She holds herself above everyone because one slip would turn her back into the daughter of a servant. Her contempt for common-born Tiye is the scorn she fears from Egypt's own nobles, turned onto someone lower.

**Integration**: This deepens canon's 'entitled, looks down on Tiye' without changing it, and it explains the trait through Tyre's history rather than through a plot event. It also sets up a quiet parallel: Isis and Tiye are both upstarts in the eyes of the old court.

### She watched the north burn (Antiquarian)

Affects: Isis, Tyre, Assyria, Ramesses III, the Assyrian war plan

**Characterization**: Born around 1205, Isis was a girl on the island when Ugarit and the northern ports fell. Tyre was the coast's harbour-master and took in wrecked ships and their crews. Ugarit's king had sent his army to help the Hittites, and the relief came only after his city had been sacked. Her lesson is that allies arrive late and a city on the sea stands alone. Assyria's raids on the dye coast look to her like the start of Ugarit again. That is why she pushes so hard for Egypt's war: she has seen what happens when a great king decides it is not his problem.

### The exported sister (Mythographer)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Isis, Isis's brother, Tyre, Ramesses III, The harem conspiracy, Tyre against Assyria

**Plot**: This flips the Dido legend. When the marriage to the new 20th-Dynasty king was arranged around 1185–1182, Isis had a real claim to rule in Tyre, either jointly with her brother or as their father's favorite. Her brother, or the merchant houses backing him, settled the question by marrying her off to Egypt. Dido had to flee Tyre with the treasure; Isis was sent out of it with a dowry, and nobody had to die. So when the coup catches her in Tyre, she is not a guest but a former rival who has come home, with a son and an Egyptian queen's title, just as Tyre needs someone to lead its war against Assyria.

**Integration**: Fits the canon that Ramesses III married her to draw northern Canaan back to Egypt: this adds Tyre's own motive for the match. It works whether her brother or a son of hers leads Tyre's war, and it raises the stakes of that open question without settling it.

Questions this raises:
- Did Isis agree to the Egyptian marriage as a bargain, or was it done to her? -> Author's answer: done to her
- Does her brother still sit on Tyre's throne in 1160, or has the claim passed to his children? (unanswered)

### The marriage contract: Great Royal Wife or nothing (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Isis, Ramesses III, Tyre, Tiye, Pi-Ramesses

**World**: Tyre's price for Isis copied Hatti's price for Maathorneferure: she had to be named Great Royal Wife, not one more foreign wife sent to the harem. Her dowry was paid in Tyre's own wealth: dyed purple wool and finished cloth, silver, cedar, and the use of Tyrian ships for Egyptian troops. Egyptian soldiers met her caravan and escorted it down the coast road. She came with a sworn Tyrian household of ladies, scribes who could write Akkadian and Canaanite, and her own priests. That household is a small Tyre inside the khener, with its own budget and its own envoys.

**Integration**: This fits canon: Ramesses III married her to pull northern Canaan back toward Egypt. Divergence: no Levantine city-state ever won Great Royal Wife rank in reality; only great kings like the Hittites did. Her household also gives Isis a ready escort and a route if she is in Tyre during the coup.

Questions this raises:
- Did Isis receive an Egyptian court name as Maathorneferure did, or did she insist on keeping her Tyrian name? (unanswered)
- Did Ramesses ever send her away to a provincial harem-palace such as Mer-wer, as some scholars think Ramesses II did with his Hittite bride? (unanswered)

### Partner in the war, not in the bed (Mythographer)

### Aegon is the evidence against her (Psychologist)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Isis, Aegon, Ramesses III, Helen

**Characterization**: Isis cannot admit Aegon is unfit, because a failed heir of her blood disproves the creed her pride rests on. So she shields him and blames everyone else: his father's coldness, his companions, the black-market purple trade. Her defense of him is self-defense dressed as a mother's love.
