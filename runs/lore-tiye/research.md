## Background (context)

### Antiquarian: Bronze Age grounding

#### What the record actually says about Tiye
- Almost nothing personal. The trial documents call her only Tiye, a lesser wife of Ramesses III. They say she wanted her son Pentawer (Pentaweret) on the throne instead of the heir who became Ramesses IV, and that she recruited officials and servants to carry messages out of the harem.
- No father, mother, birthplace or titles are recorded for her, and her punishment is not recorded. The real historical gap is total, so the canon's Olenna and Tywin options contradict no evidence.
- The trial record renamed many defendants with insulting pseudonyms. Mesedsure, for example, means 'Re hates him'. Their real names were wiped out, so in the real record Tiye's family may also have been erased.
- Name clash to be aware of: Ramesses III's own mother was a queen called Tiy-Merenese. In the real court, 'Tiye' was the name of the king's mother.
- One popular account says Ramesses III named no heir and left the Great Royal Wife cartouches blank, which encouraged the plot. Treat this as one writer's interpretation, not as settled.

#### The khener (royal harem) as an institution
- Egyptian terms: ipet, kheneret and, in the New Kingdom, per-kheneret ('house of the khener'). Scholars use 'harem' as an umbrella for the royal women, their circle, and the institutions and places tied to them. Khener comes from a verb meaning to restrain or confine.
- It was not just a bedchamber. It was a large, privileged and largely self-sufficient community. Harem-towns such as Mer-wer (Gurob, at the mouth of the Fayum), founded by Thutmose III, ran their own economy.
- Weaving was its industry. A text from Seti II's reign has a senior harem official boasting of the fine garments it made for the king.
- Who lived there: wives, 'royal ornaments' (lower-ranking women of the king), singers and musicians, royal children, wet-nurses, weavers and foreign brides. Ramesses II's Hittite wife is attested at Mer-wer.
- Staff: overseers of the harem, harem scribes, inspectors and doorkeepers. The doorkeepers' wives appear among the convicted conspirators.
- The plot seems to have been based in the harem at Pi-Ramesses, where the harem scribe Pairy had a house. One reconstruction places the planned killing at the Opet festival in Thebes.

#### How non-royal women reached the king
- The best analogue for the Tywin option is Yuya. He was Master of Horse, the king's lieutenant of chariotry, and a wealthy local notable of Akhmim with priestly offices for Min. His daughter, also named Tiye, became Amenhotep III's Great Royal Wife. His wife Tjuya is described by some scholars as a servant of the queen mother's household. The family was not royal but held high office, which makes 'commoner' a simplification.
- The analogue for the Olenna option is the nursery and the nurses. Royal children were raised in the kap (nursery) alongside sons of favoured officials and of foreign vassals, who carried the title 'child of the kap' for life. Royal nurses were honoured enough to appear in their sons' tombs, as with the nurse of Amenhotep II in the tomb of Qenamun.
- Military reward: a soldier's deeds before the king earned the 'gold of valour', paid repeatedly 'in the sight of the whole land', together with captives as slaves and grants of fields. Ahmose son of Ebana is the classic case.

#### Sherden as a landholding class
- Ramesses II took defeated Sherden raiders into his personal guard. Reliefs show them with horned helmets topped by a disc, round shields, and long swords or dirks, possibly of the Naue II type.
- The Wilbour Papyrus is a land survey of Middle Egypt from Ramesses V's reign, just after this story's period. It names some 42 individual Sherden landholders. In one sample of 132 village plot-holders, 21 were soldiers and 8 were Sherden. Retainers of Sherden officers also appear as holders.
- Plots in this register were heritable, most often 3 or 5 arouras. This is the documented basis for 'service passes from father to son.'
- Divergence already in canon: historically the Sherden also fought against Egypt among the Sea Peoples. Canon makes them a loyal corps separate from the Greek raiders.

#### How the historical plot used family outside the walls
- The chief of the chamber Pebekkamen 'began to bring out their word to their mothers and their brothers'. The harem women's families outside were the plot's reach into the world. Those relatives were told to stir up the people and incite hostility against the king.
- The captain of the Nubian (Kush) archers and other high officials were won over. Standard translations say he was drawn in by a letter from his sister in the harem; I could not verify that detail here.
- Other means: a magic roll taken from the king's own library, wax figures and charms smuggled in to disable guards, and a pass or official writing material from the treasury to get access.
- The trials: twelve magistrates heard five trials. About 28 people were executed and others, including Pentawer, were allowed to kill themselves. Two judges and a police captain who caroused with the accused women lost their ears and noses.

### Psychologist: character friction

#### Analogue: the real harem conspiracy
- In the historical record (the Judicial Papyrus of Turin and related papyri), Tiye's plot drew in harem officials, butlers, scribes and at least one army officer. Women in the khener wrote to brothers who commanded troops outside the palace and urged them to rise. One commander was posted in Kush. Wax figures and written charms were reportedly used to weaken the guards.
- A 2012 CT scan showed that Ramesses III's throat had been cut. Pentawer was tried and allowed to take his own life. The body known as 'Unknown Man E' is often linked to him.
- DIVERGENCE: here the plot succeeds and Pentawer is crowned. One detail of the real case still matters psychologically: the plot ran on the ties of the khener, meaning household staff, kin and favours, more than on armies. It was a conspiracy of people the court looked straight past.

#### How the khener worked
- The khener was a working institution as well as the royal women's quarters. It had its own estates, weaving workshops, granaries, overseers, scribes and doorkeepers. Some harem-palaces stood away from the capital, such as Mer-wer (Gurob) at the Faiyum.
- Its staff included the handmaidens, nurses and stewards a common girl's mother could plausibly have been. A handmaiden would know its back corridors, its debts and its gossip.
- A secondary wife there had income and servants of her own, but her rank was granted rather than inherited. Her son was a 'king's son' with no path to the throne while a Great Royal Wife's son lived.

#### Tiye's timeline, derived from canon
- Born around 1190, the first year of the War of Succession. Her earliest years were spent in civil war, and she was about 5 when Setnakhte's line took the throne.
- About 13 at Ramesses III's Year 8 victory over the Sea Peoples (around 1177). If the Tywin origin is chosen, a life saved in that battle or in the Libyan wars fits the two years before her marriage almost exactly.
- Married at about 15 (around 1175), when Isis had already been Great Royal Wife for close to a decade and Aegon was a small child. She bore Pentawer almost at once.
- About 30 in 1160. She has spent half her life in the khener.
- The Sherden wrinkle: canon calls Sherden families hereditary and 'well-off rather than super rich', and ordinary Egyptians lump them with foreign invaders. If Tywin is Sherden-born, Tiye's 'common birth' would mean soldier-class and part foreign, not peasant. That echoes Isis's own fear of being exposed as a vassal's daughter.

#### Tiye
- **Core want:** Pentawer on the throne, and never again to be looked down on.
- **Core need:** to stop measuring her worth by the court's eyes, Isis's above all. No crown can give her that.
- **The lie she tells herself:** 'I am doing this for my son.' In truth she is settling the account of the fifteen-year-old who believed that being chosen meant being kept.
- **Competing obligations:** she owes her son a future, her family the fortune they rose on, and her allies the war-money they want saved. She also has whatever loyalty she still feels to the man who raised her up. She cannot honour all of these.
- **Pressure points:** any sign that Pentawer does not want the crown; real tenderness from Ramesses III arriving too late; the nobles treating her as disposable; a moment when Isis treats her as an equal.
- **Justifying her worst act:** 'He took a girl and gave her nothing to stand on. I have killed one man and no children, which is more mercy than any king has shown.' The rule that no children die is her proof to herself that she is not a monster.
- **Shrewdness not yet on the page:** fifteen years of being overlooked trained her in the khener's skills: patience, favours, servants and silence. Her cunning works through the people nobody watches.

#### Tiye's surviving parent (Olenna or Tywin, open)
- Under either origin, Tiye was offered. Olenna would have pushed her out of ambition; Tywin would have presented her as a gift of gratitude. The family's present wealth and rank are the price paid for her.
- **Want:** to keep and grow what the marriage bought.
- **Lie:** 'We gave her a better life.'
- **Pressure point:** the coup risks everything the parent has gained, so the parent is both Tiye's most natural ally and her most likely betrayer.

#### Pentawer
- He is about the age Tiye was when she was given away. After the coup he will marry an Assyrian princess for policy, exactly as his mother and Isis were married.
- He is the conspiracy's object, and canon does not yet say whether he is also an author. His inner life is mostly unwritten. The key question is how much he knows.

#### Ramesses III, as Tiye sees him
- His own royal blood is thin, because his line runs through Setnakhte. He therefore cannot let blood stop mattering. If a commoner's capable son were allowed to outrank an unfit heir of better blood, his own claim would lose its footing.
- His contempt for Tiye's birth is his own insecurity aimed at someone below him, the same move Isis makes.

#### Isis, as Tiye's mirror
- Both women are upstarts in the old court's eyes, and neither admits it.
- Isis's weapon is form: she will not grant a rank she has not given. Tiye's grievance is about exactly that form.

#### Friction map
- **Tiye and Isis:** two upstarts. Each despises in the other what she fears is true of herself. Isis's refusal to address Tiye by rank is the wound Tiye wants healed by power.
- **Tiye and Ramesses III:** she gave him her youth and a son, and he never gave the son a road. Whatever she once felt for him has soured into a debt.
- **Tiye and her surviving parent:** they need each other and resent each other. The parent's rise is the price of her marriage, and the parent's men or position may be the coup's muscle.
- **Tiye and Pentawer:** she shields him and uses him at the same time. He is the boy-image of her own fifteen-year-old self.
- **Tiye and the nobles or the Littlefinger figure:** they think she is their instrument, and she knows they will drop her once the war-money is saved. A shared common birth with the Littlefinger figure (open) could make them allies or rivals.
- **Tiye and her lover (open):** if he is a rising Sherden soldier, he is the only person who chose her rather than being assigned to her. That makes him both her weakness and her proof that she is wanted.
- **Tiye and Aegon:** he is her living argument that blood does not make a king, so she needs him to stay a failure. His redemption would undercut her case.
- **Tiye and Jon:** if her parent is a Sherden commander, Jon's training is in her family's hands. That gives her leverage over keeping him away.
- **Tiye and the Assyrian princess (future):** the third foreign or upstart bride, brought in by Tiye herself.

### Historian: historical analogues

#### The other Tiye: Queen Tiye, wife of Amenhotep III, and her parents Yuya and Thuya (c. 1390 BCE)
- **What happened.** Around two centuries before the story, a common-born girl from the provincial cult town of Akhmim became Great Royal Wife of Amenhotep III. Her parents, Yuya and Thuya, were not royal. Yuya was a wealthy Akhmim landowner.
- **Yuya's titles** were "King's Lieutenant", "Master of the Horse" and "God's Father". One account adds "Commander of the Chariotry" and "Overseer of the Oxen of Min". He was also a priest of Min. In other words, he was a chariot-corps officer who also had a foothold in a temple.
- **Thuya** held religious titles. One source describes her as superintendent of the harem (the god's musician-women) of Amun and of Min. Her career ran through a different kind of khener: the temple's household of women.
- **How it worked.** The marriage was publicised on commemorative scarabs that named her commoner parents openly, with no attempt to invent a royal pedigree. Her parents probably sat on a regency council during the king's youth. Her brother Anen became Second Prophet of Amun, and Ay, who later became pharaoh, is widely believed (but not proven) to be another brother.
- **Outcome.** Tiye became a political power in her own right. She dealt directly with foreign kings, and Mitanni's king wrote to her. Her family turned into a dynastic bloc: officers and priests at first, and in time a member who took the throne.
- **What transfers.** The historical record already has the two candidate origins in a single household: a chariot-officer father with a warrior's claim on the king, and a mother rooted in a harem institution. A common-born family does not have to choose between sword and harem. It rises through both, and sons and brothers then take temple and army posts.

#### Wu Zetian and the founder's merchant (Tang China, 7th century CE)
- **Family.** Wu's father, Wu Shiyue, was a fairly well-off timber merchant. Li Yuan, the future Tang founder, stayed at the Wu house several times while posted in the region. After Li Yuan overthrew the Sui, he rewarded the family with grain, land and wealth, and gave Wu Shiyue governorships. The family's whole fortune was a gift from the founder of the new dynasty.
- **Mother.** Lady Yang came from a powerful family distantly related to the fallen Sui imperial house. She had her daughter educated, which was unusual for girls at the time. When Wu was summoned to the palace at about 14, her mother wept. Wu answered: "How do you know that it is not my fortune to meet the Son of Heaven?"
- **Mechanics of the rise.** Wu entered as a fifth-rank consort with secretarial duties. She was sent to a temple when Taizong died, brought back by Empress Wang to use against a rival consort, bore sons, and accused the empress of murder and witchcraft. The senior chancellors resisted her elevation. Her mother personally lobbied one of them and was rebuffed. Wu became empress in 655 and later had both rivals killed.
- **What transfers.** A new-money family whose rise is owed entirely to a dynasty's founder: compare Setnakhte founding the 20th Dynasty. One parent is upwardly mobile and the other carries faded high blood and teaches the daughter to read and to want. The old officeholders' resistance is the barrier the daughter learns to get around.

#### Theodora: the bear-keeper's daughter (Byzantium, 6th century CE)
- **Family.** Her father, Acacius, kept the bears for the Green faction at the Hippodrome and died when she was a child. Her mother, a dancer, remarried. A Green official was bribed to give the stepfather's post to someone else, leaving the family destitute. The mother put her small daughters before the Greens as suppliants and was refused. The rival Blues took pity and gave them a place.
- **Mechanics.** Theodora went on the stage. To marry her, Justinian needed his uncle to issue a new law letting senators marry former actresses, and the empress Euphemia opposed the match until she died. The aristocratic historian Procopius later wrote her up as a whore, and some historians read that as elite fear of her power.
- **Family once in power.** Her elder sister Comito married the general Sittas, probably a match Theodora arranged. She also tried to marry her grandson into Belisarius's family. During the Nika riots she steadied Justinian, and one source says she insisted on the execution of the rival claimant.
- **What transfers.** For a common-born family, patronage is the whole economy. Formative humiliation comes from patrons who refuse to help. Once a family is raised, it binds generals to itself by marrying off sisters and grandchildren. The legal barrier to the marriage is a precise, writable obstacle.

#### Hürrem and Nur Jahan: the favourite's family becomes a faction, then splits
- **Hürrem (Ottoman, 16th century).** She was a Ruthenian captive taken in a slave raid who became Suleiman's favourite. She was freed and married in 1534, against precedent, and broke the custom that limited each concubine to one son. Her daughter Mihrimah married Rüstem Pasha, who became grand vizier, an alliance historians link to securing the succession for her sons. Ibrahim Pasha, who backed the eldest prince Mustafa, was executed, and Mustafa himself was executed in 1553. The claims of her direct role rest mostly on later sources. She had no birth family at court, so she built one through marriage.
- **Nur Jahan (Mughal, 17th century).** Her father became chief minister with the title Itimad-ud-Daulah, and her brother Asaf Khan became Mir Bakhshi. Her faction, sometimes called the "junta", is partly a construction of European observers. While her parents lived they restrained her. After they died, she and her brother split over the succession: Asaf Khan backed his own son-in-law, Shah Jahan, and Nur Jahan's candidate was executed.
- **What transfers.** A queen's birth family is an ally only as long as their interests match hers. Once the family holds offices of its own, a brother or father may prefer another candidate, often a son-in-law. A parent's death can remove the restraint, and also the one person who held the family together.

#### The real harem conspiracy: who Tiye actually recruited
- **Personnel.** The Turin Judicial Papyrus records Tiye's network as household staff, not grandees. A pantry chief (Pebekkamen) spread the call to action, alongside a butler, a cattle overseer, the overseer of the harem and a harem clerk. The overseer of the treasury was approached for a pass to reach the king. Six wives of men at the harem gate are among the accused. Payis, leader of the Kush troops, also appears.
- **The kin channel.** Messages went out to relatives outside, "their mothers and their brothers". Binemwese, formerly captain of archers in Nubia, was condemned because of a letter from "his sister, who was in the harem": "Incite the people to hostility! And come thou to begin hostility against thy lord".
- **Design.** The attack was to come from inside the harem, supported by a revolt outside. Ramesses III's mummy shows his throat was cut to the bone. Historically the plot failed: 28 were executed and Pentawer was allowed to take his own life.
- **What transfers.** The harem conspiracy was a kin network. Women inside the khener wrote to brothers commanding troops on the frontier, and the access points were the staff who controlled food, doors and passes. The historical sources say nothing about Tiye's own family. The only kin-based recruitment on record is another harem woman's brother in Nubia.

### Mythographer: fictional analogues

#### Bathsheba and Solomon (1 Kings 1, scripture read as narrative)
Label: biblical narrative, not history.

- **What happens:** David is old and failing. His eldest surviving son, Adonijah, starts behaving like the heir in public, with chariots and runners. The prophet Nathan goes to Bathsheba and gives her a script: remind the king of an oath that Solomon will reign. Then Nathan walks in and confirms her story. Solomon is anointed that same day.
- **What the device does:** a wife with no birthright wins the throne for her son with one well-timed scene at the dying king's side, not with an army. The text never shows David making the promise she quotes, so readers cannot tell whether it was real or invented.
- **Why it works:** readers can see both an underdog mother defending her child and a fabricated succession at once. A Catholic commentary notes that Bathsheba is also acting against her own replacement at the king's side.
- **Family layer:** Bathsheba came into the palace from a soldier's household. Her first husband, Uriah the Hittite, was a foreign fighter in David's army.
- **Pitfalls:** treating her as a passive beauty (the bathing scene) or as a stock seductress. Solomon also has Adonijah killed afterward, which the canon rules out for this story.

#### The Woodvilles in Shakespeare's Richard III (drama)
Label: drama, built on a hostile Tudor tradition.

- **What it does:** the queen's lowborn rise pulls her whole family up with her, and the family becomes the target. Richard sneers that "every Jack became a gentleman." Margaret calls the kin's titles a "fire-new stamp of honor" that is "scarce current." Elizabeth answers that their scorn is envy of her family's advancement.
- **Why it works:** the old nobility's snobbery reads as both justified and ugly, and the queen's kin act as a weathervane: you can measure her power by what happens to them. The real coup began with the arrest of her brother and her son, Rivers and Grey, before anyone touched the crown.
- **Pitfalls:** in the play the kin are mostly interchangeable victims with no inner lives, and the "grasping in-laws" device turns easily into class caricature.

#### The Boleyns in Wolf Hall (novel)
Label: historical fiction.

- **What it does:** Mantel's Thomas Boleyn is an ambitious, unscrupulous father who uses both daughters to climb. When Mary Boleyn, the elder, is set aside, the family moves straight on to Anne. Study guides point to the uncle, Norfolk, as the family's real engine, outside the Boleyn name.
- **Why it works:** it changes the question from "is she a schemer?" to "who is spending her?" The daughter becomes a currency that her family keeps cashing in.
- **Pitfalls:** the "scheming family pushes a daughter at the king" plot has been done many times (The Other Boleyn Girl, The Tudors), and the daughter can shrink into a pawn with no inner life.

#### Tywin and Olenna (A Song of Ice and Fire / Game of Thrones)
Label: fantasy fiction. These are the canon's own placeholder names.

- **Tywin:** a patriarch who treats his children as pieces in a family legacy. He is a strong engine but very recognisable, and readers will bring his whole arc with them.
- **Olenna:** an old woman whose wit hides the fact that she is the killer. She poisoned Joffrey at his own wedding and reveals it only as she dies: "Tell Cersei. I want her to know it was me".
- **Why they work:** each one hides real power behind a public role (the general, the harmless grandmother).
- **Pitfalls:** the names and quips are now memes. Copying the type wholesale invites "Egyptian Lannisters" readings. The fix is to keep the function and drop the surface.

#### Lady Jessica (Dune, novel)
Label: science fiction.

- **What it does:** Jessica is Duke Leto's concubine, never his wife. He keeps her unmarried so he stays free for a political marriage. Her order told her to bear a daughter. She chose to bear a son, and that one private act starts the whole saga.
- **Why it works:** a woman of secondary rank turns out to hold the decisive hidden agency, and her status is a deliberate political choice by the man she loves.
- **Pitfalls:** "she did it for love" can make her agency look small. Her institution, the Bene Gesserit, can take over her story.

#### Livia (I, Claudius, novel and BBC series)
Label: historical fiction. Historians reject most of Graves's poisonings.

- **What it does:** a ruler's wife clears the heirs in front of her son, mostly by poison. One heir, Postumus, is instead framed for a sexual crime and exiled to an island.
- **Why it works:** a smiling domestic surface hides a body count.
- **Pitfalls and relevance:** the poisoning-matriarch cliché is worn out, and it clashes with this canon, where Tiye needs the princes away, not dead. Postumus, removed by disgrace and exile rather than murder, is the nearer model.

#### History, not fiction: the real harem conspiracy
- In the historical record Tiye was probably a minor wife. She wanted Pentawer to replace the designated heir and had little trouble finding officials to help her.
- A pantry chief, Pebekkamen, carried the call to rebel outside the palace. He spoke to "their mothers and their brothers" of the harem women.
- Officials were also punished for consorting with the accused women.
- **Divergence:** historically the plot failed and the plotters were tried. In this canon it succeeds.

## Proposals

### The deed and the door: both parents made the match (Mythographer)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Tiye, Tywin, Olenna, Ramesses III, Isis

**Plot**: Do not choose between the two origins. Use both, and give each parent half the work, as the Boleyn father and the Norfolk uncle share it in Wolf Hall, or Bathsheba and Nathan in 1 Kings. Tywin, a common soldier, really did save Ramesses III's life, perhaps at the Year 8 battle in the Delta mouths around 1177. That deed got the family a reward and an audience, nothing more. Olenna, a servant in the khener, turned the audience into a bed. She put her 13-year-old where the king would see her again, and over the next two years worked her into a marriage, so Tiye wed at 15. The court tells the soldier's story, because it flatters the king. The truth is the handmaid's.

**Integration**: This fits the timeline: Tiye marries around 1175 at about 15, two years after the Year 8 victory. It turns the open either/or into a both/and. It still leaves open which parent is alive, and leaves room for Tywin as a general.

### Olenna's khener: a common woman's ladder (Historian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Olenna, Tiye, the khener at Pi-Ramesses, the harem conspiracy

**World**: In this world the khener is a staffed institution and a career, not just a set of wives. It has overseers, clerks, chantresses, gatekeepers whose wives live inside, and pantry and wardrobe officers. A common-born woman can climb it, as Thuya did in the god's harem. Olenna rose from handmaiden to an overseer of the women's household, and her daughter came to the king's notice through that office. Even if Olenna is dead, Tiye inherits her mother's people: the pantry chief, the clerk, the gate-wives. Those are exactly the people the real conspiracy ran on.

**Integration**: This keeps both candidate origins at once: a warrior father, and a mother who pushed her daughter through a harem office. It also gives Tiye a concrete conspiracy network drawn straight from the Turin papyrus. It does not decide whether Olenna is alive.

### Tywin as a Sherden plot-holder, rewarded Yuya-fashion (Antiquarian)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Tiye, Tywin, Sherden, Ramesses III, Mer-wer

**World**: This version roots Tiye's family in the documented Sherden landholding class. They hold heritable plots of 3–5 arouras in Middle Egypt near the harem-town of Mer-wer at the mouth of the Fayum, so they are well-off farmers in service, not nobles. Tywin's deed before the king brings the standard rewards: gold of valour given in public, captives, extra fields. The prize no one else gets is his daughter's entry into the khener, the way the chariot officer Yuya's daughter rose to queen. This gives the family new gold and new land, but still Sherden-farmer manners, which is exactly what the old families would sneer at.

**Integration**: This resolves q-8518cf12 toward Tywin, and q-fc8dfe3b toward Egypt (Middle Egypt) rather than southern Canaan. The timing fits: the Year 8 Delta battle (~1177) falls two years before Tiye's marriage (~1175). ### Tiye keeps Pentawer clean, as Isis shields Aegon (Psychologist)
Status: ACCEPTED by the author (treat as settled)
Affects: Tiye, Pentawer, Isis, Aegon, Assyrian princess

**Characterization**: Tiye deliberately keeps Pentawer ignorant of the plot. Partly this is so he can never be called a parricide. Partly it is because she wants him to stay the innocent she was at fifteen. She calls it protection.
